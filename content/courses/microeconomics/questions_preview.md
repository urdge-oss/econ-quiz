## Level 1 · Market Forces — Supply, demand & elasticity
Exam questions 1–8 · 23 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- Price moves toward equilibrium: excess demand bids it up, excess supply pushes it down, until Qd = Qs.
- A change in the good's own price moves you ALONG a curve. Anything else (costs, technology, incomes, other prices) SHIFTS the curve.
- Elasticity = % change in quantity ÷ % change in price. It is not the slope: on a straight demand line it changes at every point.
- Time matters: demand for nondurables and supply of commodities are more elastic in the long run; demand for durables is more elastic in the short run.
- A binding price ceiling turns a temporary excess demand into a lasting shortage, so something other than price does the rationing.

**L01-001** · one answer · difficulty 1 · exam Q1  
The market price of copper is above its equilibrium level. What brings it back to equilibrium?

- ✅ Excess supply builds up, so sellers cut prices until Qd = Qs
- ▫️ Buyers bid prices higher because they fear the surplus will soon run out
- ▫️ Producers raise output until the extra supply clears at the higher price
- ▫️ The demand curve shifts right until it crosses supply at the current price

> Above equilibrium Qs > Qd, so there is excess supply. Competition among sellers pushes the price down, which raises Qd and lowers Qs until they meet. Nothing shifts — the adjustment is a movement along both curves.

**L01-002** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q1  
A strike unexpectedly closes several large copper mines. In a competitive world market, what happens?

- ▫️ Demand shifts right because buyers stock up, so both price and quantity rise
- ✅ Supply shifts left; at the old price there is excess demand, so price rises and quantity falls
- ▫️ Supply shifts left, but the price stays put because a world market absorbs the shock
- ▫️ Supply moves along the curve to a lower quantity while the price stays the same

> Lost mine output is a supply shift, not a price-driven movement. At the old price buyers want more than is available; the excess demand is temporary because the price rises until the market clears at a higher P and lower Q.

**L01-003** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q1, Q8  
What separates the excess demand after a supply disruption from the shortage caused by a binding price ceiling?

- ▫️ They are the same thing: both last until the government ends the crisis
- ✅ With a ceiling the price is not allowed to rise, so the gap between Qd and Qs persists
- ▫️ A ceiling raises quantity supplied, while a disruption reduces it
- ▫️ After a disruption the gap is larger, so it takes longer for the market to clear

> In a free market, excess demand is self-correcting — the price rises and closes the gap. A legal maximum price blocks that mechanism, so the shortage lasts as long as the ceiling does.

**L01-004** · select all · difficulty 1 · exam Q2  
Which events SHIFT the world supply curve of crude oil, rather than causing a movement along it?

- ▫️ A fall in the oil price makes producers cap some marginal wells
- ▫️ Strong demand from China raises the world oil price and producers pump more
- ✅ A war shuts down pipelines in a major producing region
- ✅ Fracking technology lowers the cost of extracting shale oil
- ▫️ Higher incomes in Asia raise demand for crude oil

> Supply shifts when something other than the oil price changes producers' willingness to sell: costs, technology, capacity, war. Higher Asian incomes shift DEMAND, not supply. When producers react to a change in the oil price itself (up or down), they move along the existing supply curve.

**L01-005** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q2  
Higher world crude prices lead US shale firms to drill and complete more wells. How is this shown on a supply diagram?

- ✅ A movement up along the existing supply curve
- ▫️ An inward shift of the demand curve
- ▫️ An outward shift of the supply curve
- ▫️ A pivot of the supply curve to make it steeper

> The trigger is the good's own price, so output rises along the existing curve. An outward shift would need a non-price change — for example, cheaper drilling technology letting firms supply more at every price.

**L01-006** · one answer · difficulty 1 · exam Q3  
Which formula defines the price elasticity of demand?

- ▫️ (ΔQ/Q) ÷ (ΔI/I)
- ▫️ ΔQ ÷ ΔP
- ✅ (ΔQ/Q) ÷ (ΔP/P)
- ▫️ (ΔP/P) ÷ (ΔQ/Q)

> Elasticity compares percentage changes, so it has no units. ΔQ/ΔP is the inverse slope, which depends on units. The last option is income elasticity.

**L01-007** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q3  
Why is price elasticity not the same thing as the slope of a straight-line demand curve?

- ▫️ They are the same, measured in different currencies
- ✅ E = (ΔQ/ΔP)·(P/Q): the slope term is constant, but the ratio P/Q changes along the line
- ▫️ Elasticity is constant along a straight line, while the slope keeps changing
- ▫️ The slope uses percentages, elasticity uses absolute units

> On a linear demand curve ΔQ/ΔP never changes, but P/Q is high near the price axis and low near the quantity axis. So demand is elastic at high prices and inelastic at low prices, even though the slope is the same everywhere.

**L01-008** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q3  
Why do economists use the arc (midpoint) elasticity when measuring elasticity between two price points?

- ▫️ It always gives a value closer to −1 than point elasticity does
- ▫️ It measures elasticity at a single point, which is more precise
- ▫️ It is the only formula that works for curved demand curves
- ✅ Using average P and Q gives the same answer whether the price rises or falls

> With the simple formula, going from P=4 to P=5 gives a different percentage change than going from 5 to 4. The arc formula uses the midpoint of P and Q, so the result is symmetric. Point elasticity is the version used at one point on the curve.

**L01-009** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q3  
Where on a straight-line demand curve is total consumer expenditure (P·Q) highest?

- ▫️ At the lowest price, where the most units are sold
- ▫️ Where the curve meets the price axis
- ▫️ Where demand is most inelastic
- ✅ At the midpoint, where demand is unit elastic

> Above the midpoint demand is elastic, so cutting the price raises spending. Below it demand is inelastic, so cutting the price lowers spending. Spending peaks where |E| = 1, which on a straight line is exactly halfway.

**L01-010** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q3  
Demand for a medicine is inelastic (E = −0.3). The price goes up 10%. What happens to total spending on it?

- ✅ It rises: quantity falls only about 3%
- ▫️ It falls, because quantity falls by about 30%
- ▫️ It stays the same, because spending never depends on price
- ▫️ It falls, because any price rise reduces revenue

> When |E| < 1, quantity falls by a smaller percentage than price rises, so P·Q goes up. This is why sellers of goods with few substitutes can raise revenue by raising prices.

**L01-011** · put in order · difficulty 3 · exam Q3  
Demand is P = 10 − Q. Put these points in order from the most elastic to the least elastic.

1. At P = 9
2. At P = 7
3. At P = 5 (the midpoint)
4. At P = 2

> For this curve E = −P/(10 − P): −9 at P = 9, about −2.3 at P = 7, −1 at the midpoint and −0.25 at P = 2. Moving down a straight demand line, demand becomes less and less elastic.

**L01-012** · one answer · difficulty 1 · exam Q4  
The cross-price elasticity between imported electric vehicles and domestic gasoline cars is +0.8. What does that mean?

- ▫️ EVs are a luxury: a 10% rise in income raises EV demand by about 8%
- ✅ They are substitutes: a 10% rise in gasoline-car prices raises EV demand by about 8%
- ▫️ They are complements: a 10% rise in gasoline-car prices lowers EV demand by about 8%
- ▫️ The goods are unrelated, because the value is below 1

> A positive cross-price elasticity means a higher price of one good raises demand for the other — the definition of substitutes. For carmakers it means price moves by rivals selling the other technology directly affect their sales.

**L01-013** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q4  
The income elasticity of demand for luxury EVs is +2.2. What does that tell a global carmaker?

- ✅ Sales grow faster than incomes and are very sensitive to the business cycle
- ▫️ Higher incomes reduce sales, because rich buyers switch to other brands
- ▫️ Sales do not respond to income, only to the price of fuel
- ▫️ Sales grow more slowly than incomes, so they are recession-proof

> An income elasticity above 1 marks a luxury: a 1% rise in income raises demand by 2.2%. Such firms do well in fast-growing, high-income markets but suffer most in recessions, so they diversify across countries whose cycles differ.

**L01-014** · select all · difficulty 2 · exam Q4  
Which pairings of elasticity value and meaning are correct?

- ▫️ Income elasticity above 1 — an inferior good
- ▫️ Cross-price elasticity of exactly 0 — perfect substitutes
- ▫️ Cross-price elasticity below 0 — substitutes
- ✅ Income elasticity between 0 and 1 — a necessity
- ✅ Income elasticity below 0 — an inferior good

> Inferior goods have negative income elasticity; necessities rise with income, but by less than income; luxuries rise more than proportionally (above 1). A negative cross-price elasticity means complements (substitutes have a positive one). A cross elasticity of 0 means the goods are unrelated.

**L01-015** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q5  
Which good's demand is more elastic in the long run than in the short run, and why?

- ▫️ Commercial aircraft — airlines can only postpone orders for a few months
- ▫️ Gasoline — in the long run drivers stop noticing price changes
- ✅ Gasoline — over time drivers switch to fuel-efficient cars and transit
- ▫️ Both have equal short-run and long-run elasticities because both are traded globally

> For a nondurable like gasoline, consumers are locked into their current car in the short run, so demand is inelastic. Given time, they change the stock of cars and habits, so long-run demand is much more elastic.

**L01-016** · one answer · difficulty 3 · exam Q5  
Why is demand for durable goods such as commercial aircraft MORE elastic in the short run than in the long run?

- ▫️ Durable goods are luxuries, and luxuries always have elastic demand
- ▫️ Manufacturers cut prices over time, so long-run demand does not need to respond
- ✅ Buyers can delay replacing old units, but the fleet must eventually be replaced
- ▫️ In the long run airlines lose interest in price because aircraft are a small share of their costs

> For durables, annual purchases replace a large existing stock. When prices rise, buyers can keep flying old planes for a while, so purchases drop sharply at first. In the long run the stock has to be maintained, so the percentage fall in demand is smaller.

**L01-017** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q6  
Why is the long-run supply of aluminium or wheat much more elastic than its short-run supply?

- ▫️ Producers ignore prices in the short run and only react once prices are reported
- ▫️ Short-run supply is limited by price controls that expire in the long run
- ✅ Capacity is fixed short-run; new mines and land take time
- ▫️ In the long run demand becomes more elastic, which makes supply more elastic too

> Supply elasticity reflects how easily output can expand. In the short run firms can only run existing capacity harder. In the long run new mines, smelters and planted acreage come on stream, so the response to a price change is much larger.

**L01-018** · one answer · difficulty 3 · exam Q6  
Copper is also supplied from recycled scrap. How does this secondary supply behave?

- ✅ More elastic in the short run: high prices pull scrap out, but the stock runs down
- ▫️ It has a negative elasticity, because high prices make people hoard old metal
- ▫️ It is perfectly inelastic, because the amount of scrap is fixed by past consumption of copper
- ▫️ It is more elastic in the long run, because recycling plants take years to build

> High prices quickly pull old wiring and pipes into recycling, so secondary supply jumps in the short run. Over time the available stock of scrap runs down, so the long-run response is smaller. In Pindyck's copper example this raises the short-run elasticity of total supply.

**L01-019** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q7  
World copper demand is QD = 32 − 0.20P. Demand rises by 20% at every price. Which equation describes the new demand?

- ▫️ QD = 32 − 0.24P
- ✅ QD = 38.4 − 0.24P
- ▫️ QD = 32.2 − 0.20P
- ▫️ QD = 38.4 − 0.20P

> "20% more at every price" means multiplying the whole function by 1.2, so both the intercept and the slope term scale: 1.2·(32 − 0.20P). Adding 20% only to the intercept would be a different (parallel) shift. Then set the new demand equal to supply and solve for P and Q.

**L01-020** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q7  
Demand for copper rises while supply is fairly inelastic. What is the likely result?

- ▫️ Quantity rises by a large percentage, price by a small one
- ▫️ Price and quantity rise by exactly the same percentage
- ▫️ Price falls, because producers expand to meet the extra demand
- ✅ Price rises by a large percentage, quantity by a small one

> When demand shifts right along a steep (inelastic) supply curve, most of the adjustment shows up in price. In the copper example, a 20% rise in demand raises the price by about 30% but quantity by only about 7%.

**L01-021** · select all · difficulty 2 · exam Q8  
A government caps the price of an imported fertiliser below the market-clearing level. Which outcomes should you expect?

- ✅ Queues, waiting lists or coupons decide who gets the fertiliser
- ✅ Importers bring in less, because the capped price no longer covers their costs
- ▫️ Imports rise, because foreign suppliers want to win market share
- ▫️ Farmers buy more fertiliser than before, because it is cheaper
- ✅ A black market appears where it sells above the legal price

**L01-022** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q8  
Who gains from a binding price ceiling on an essential import?

- ▫️ All buyers, because everyone pays less
- ▫️ The government, because it collects the price difference as revenue
- ✅ Only buyers who actually obtain the good at the lower price
- ▫️ Importers, because they sell more units

> Lucky buyers who get the good pay less and gain. Buyers who are rationed out lose, and so do sellers, who sell fewer units at a lower price. A ceiling raises no revenue — it is a transfer plus a deadweight loss.

**L01-023** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q1, Q8 📊 ceiling  
On the graph, the price ceiling is Pmax. What quantity do consumers actually buy?

- ▫️ Q₂
- ▫️ Q₀
- ✅ Q₁
- ▫️ Q₂ − Q₁

## Level 2 · Taste Test — Preferences, budgets & optimal choice
Exam questions 9–16 · 25 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- Four assumptions: completeness, transitivity, more is better, diminishing MRS (convex indifference curves).
- MRS = how much Y you would give up for one more X and stay equally happy = |slope of the indifference curve| = MUx/MUy.
- Budget line: I = Px·X + Py·Y, slope −Px/Py. Income changes shift it; price changes rotate it.
- Interior optimum: MRS = Px/Py, or MUx/Px = MUy/Py. If no tangency exists, you get a corner solution.
- Choices reveal preferences (WARP). Laspeyres overstates cost-of-living increases; Paasche understates them.

**L02-001** · select all · difficulty 1 · exam Q9  
Which are standard assumptions about consumer preferences in Pindyck & Rubinfeld?

- ✅ Completeness — the consumer can rank any two bundles
- ✅ Diminishing MRS — indifference curves are convex
- ✅ More is better — goods are desirable
- ▫️ Cardinality — utility can be measured in fixed units
- ✅ Transitivity — if X beats Y and Y beats Z, then X beats Z

> Consumer theory needs only an ordinal ranking. Utility numbers just order bundles; their size has no meaning. The four listed assumptions give downward-sloping, non-crossing, convex indifference curves.

**L02-002** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q9  
Why can two indifference curves never cross?

- ▫️ Because the budget line can only be tangent to one indifference curve at a time
- ✅ One of two equally good bundles would contain more of both goods
- ▫️ Because indifference curves must be parallel by definition
- ▫️ Because utility cannot be measured, so curves cannot meet

> If curves U₁ and U₂ crossed at A, then A ~ B (on U₁) and A ~ D (on U₂), so by transitivity B ~ D. But one of B or D contains more of both goods, so by "more is better" it must be preferred. That contradiction rules out crossing.

**L02-003** · one answer · difficulty 1 · exam Q9  
A consumer prefers tea to coffee and coffee to juice, but juice to tea. Which assumption is violated?

- ▫️ More is better
- ▫️ Completeness
- ✅ Transitivity
- ▫️ Diminishing MRS

> Transitivity requires that if tea beats coffee and coffee beats juice, tea must beat juice. Circular rankings make a consistent "best" choice impossible.

**L02-004** · one answer · difficulty 1 · exam Q10  
What is the marginal rate of substitution of X for Y?

- ✅ The most Y the consumer would give up for one more X and stay equally happy
- ▫️ The extra utility from one more unit of X
- ▫️ The rate at which the market lets the consumer swap Y for X, given by the price ratio
- ▫️ The ratio of the price of X to the price of Y

> MRS is a preference concept: the absolute slope of the indifference curve. The market exchange rate is the price ratio Px/Py (slope of the budget line). The two are equal at an interior optimum.

**L02-005** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q10  
Why is MRS = MUx/MUy?

- ▫️ Because MRS is defined as the ratio of total utilities
- ▫️ Because the budget line slope equals the marginal utility of income
- ✅ Utility is constant, so MUx·ΔX = −MUy·ΔY
- ▫️ Because consumers always equate marginal utility to price

> Moving along an indifference curve, the utility gained from extra X must exactly offset the utility lost from less Y. Rearranging gives the MRS as the ratio of marginal utilities.

**L02-006** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q10  
What does a diminishing MRS mean?

- ▫️ The consumer's total utility falls as they buy more X
- ▫️ The price of X falls as the consumer buys more of it
- ▫️ The consumer will always give up the same amount of Y for each extra X
- ✅ The more X a consumer already has, the less Y they will give up for an extra unit of X

> As X becomes plentiful and Y scarce, X is valued less relative to Y. That makes indifference curves flatten as you move right — they are convex to the origin, which reflects a taste for balanced bundles.

**L02-007** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q11  
Two goods are perfect substitutes (one-for-one). What do the indifference curves look like?

- ✅ Straight lines with a constant MRS
- ▫️ Curves convex to the origin with diminishing MRS
- ▫️ Upward-sloping lines
- ▫️ L-shapes with a corner on a 45° ray

> If the consumer always trades one unit of X for one of Y, the MRS is constant at 1. The optimum is usually a corner: buy only the cheaper good, unless the price ratio equals the MRS.

**L02-008** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q11  
Left and right shoes are perfect complements. What follows for consumer choice?

- ✅ L-shaped curves; shoes are bought in pairs and prices cause no substitution
- ▫️ Indifference curves slope upward; extra shoes reduce utility
- ▫️ Indifference curves are straight lines; the consumer buys only the cheaper shoe
- ▫️ Indifference curves are vertical lines; only right shoes matter

> An extra left shoe without a right one adds nothing, so utility depends on the number of pairs (Leontief preferences). The optimum is at the kink, and a price change only has an income effect, never a substitution effect.

**L02-009** · one answer · difficulty 3 · exam Q11  
X is industrial pollution (a "bad") and Y is a normal good. What shape do the indifference curves have?

- ▫️ Horizontal: pollution does not affect utility
- ▫️ L-shaped: pollution and Y are consumed in fixed proportion
- ✅ Upward-sloping: more pollution must be compensated with more Y
- ▫️ Downward-sloping and convex, as with normal goods

> Keeping utility constant while adding a bad requires more of the good, so the curves slope upward. Utility increases toward the upper-left (less pollution, more Y). A rational consumer chooses zero of the bad when possible.

**L02-010** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q11  
X on the horizontal axis is a neutral good — the consumer does not care about it. What do the indifference curves look like, and what is chosen?

- ▫️ Vertical lines; the consumer spends the whole budget on X
- ▫️ L-shapes; the consumer buys X and Y in fixed proportion
- ▫️ Straight downward lines; the consumer buys equal amounts
- ✅ Horizontal lines; the consumer spends the whole budget on Y

> Utility depends only on Y, so any bundle with the same Y is equally good — a horizontal line. The MRS of X for Y is zero, so the consumer buys no X and puts all income into Y.

**L02-011** · one answer · difficulty 1 · exam Q12  
What is the slope of the budget line I = Px·X + Py·Y, with X on the horizontal axis?

- ▫️ −I/Px
- ▫️ −Py/Px
- ✅ −Px/Py
- ▫️ −MUx/MUy

> Solving for Y gives Y = I/Py − (Px/Py)·X. The slope is the market rate at which Y can be traded for X. −MUx/MUy is the slope of an indifference curve.

**L02-012** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q12  
All prices rise by 20% while nominal income stays the same. How does the budget line change?

- ▫️ It shifts outward, because nominal income now buys more
- ▫️ It rotates around the Y-intercept and becomes steeper
- ✅ Parallel inward shift: the slope is unchanged, the intercepts fall
- ▫️ It does not change, because relative prices and the price ratio are still the same

> The price ratio Px/Py is unchanged, so the slope is the same. But real income falls: the intercepts I/(1.2Px) and I/(1.2Py) move toward the origin. Only if nominal income also rose by 20% would the line stay where it was.

**L02-013** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q12  
A 10% import tariff is applied only to imported good X (on the horizontal axis). How does the budget line change?

- ▫️ It rotates around the X-intercept and becomes flatter
- ✅ It pivots inward around the Y-intercept and gets steeper
- ▫️ It rotates outward around the X-intercept
- ▫️ It shifts inward in parallel, because the consumer is poorer after the tariff

> The most Y you can buy (I/Py) is unchanged, so the Y-intercept stays. The most X falls to I/(1.1·Px), so the line pivots inward and gets steeper, because X is now relatively more expensive.

**L02-014** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q13  
At the consumer's current bundle, MRS > Px/Py. What should the consumer do?

- ▫️ Buy less X and more Y, until the MRS rises to the price ratio
- ▫️ Nothing — any bundle on the budget line is optimal
- ✅ Buy more X and less Y, until the MRS falls to the price ratio
- ▫️ Spend more than their income on X

> MRS > Px/Py means the consumer values one more X (in units of Y) more than the market charges for it. Swapping Y for X raises utility, and as X rises the MRS diminishes until it equals Px/Py.

**L02-015** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q13  
Which condition is equivalent to MRS = Px/Py at an interior optimum?

- ▫️ Px·X = Py·Y
- ▫️ MUx/MUy = Py/Px
- ✅ MUx/Px = MUy/Py
- ▫️ MUx = MUy

> Since MRS = MUx/MUy, the tangency condition becomes MUx/MUy = Px/Py, or MUx/Px = MUy/Py: the last dollar spent on each good must give the same extra utility (the equal marginal principle).

**L02-016** · one answer · difficulty 1 · exam Q13 📊 consumerChoice  
Which bundle on the graph maximises utility given the budget line?

- ▫️ C, because it has the most of good X
- ✅ A, where the indifference curve is tangent to the budget line
- ▫️ B, because it has the most of good Y
- ▫️ Any point on U₃, because it gives the highest utility

**L02-017** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q13 📊 consumerChoice  
At point B the MRS is Y/X = 16/2 = 8, while Px/Py = 2. What should this consumer do?

- ▫️ Stay at B, because it is on the budget line
- ▫️ Give up some X to buy more Y, moving up the budget line
- ✅ Give up some Y to buy more X, moving down the budget line toward A
- ▫️ Cut total spending so the MRS falls to 2

> At B the consumer would give up 8 units of Y for one more X, but the market asks only 2. Trading Y for X raises utility, and as the consumer moves toward A the MRS falls until it equals 2.

**L02-018** · select all · difficulty 2 · exam Q9, Q12, Q13 📊 consumerChoice  
Which statements about the graph are correct?

- ▫️ C is preferred to B because it contains more of good X
- ✅ At C the consumer values X less than its market price, so should buy less X
- ✅ Bundles on U₃ are preferred but not affordable
- ▫️ A costs more than B because it is on a higher indifference curve

**L02-019** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q14  
What is a corner solution?

- ✅ An optimum with zero of one good
- ▫️ An optimum at the kink of an L-shaped indifference curve
- ▫️ A bundle where the consumer spends exactly half of income on each good
- ▫️ Any bundle where the budget line is tangent to an indifference curve

> If the consumer values X less than its price at every bundle (MRS < Px/Py even with zero X), no tangency exists and they spend everything on Y. Corner solutions are typical with perfect substitutes or when one good is strongly preferred.

**L02-020** · one answer · difficulty 3 · exam Q14  
A traveller sees domestic and foreign airlines as perfect substitutes. A domestic ticket costs $300 and a foreign one $250. What does the traveller choose?

- ▫️ Pick any combination, because both airlines give exactly the same utility per flight
- ▫️ Split flights evenly to equalise marginal utility
- ✅ Fly only the foreign airline, since it is cheaper and a perfect substitute
- ▫️ Fly only the domestic airline, because quality is the same

> With straight-line indifference curves (MRS = 1), the consumer compares the MRS with the price ratio. Since the foreign ticket is cheaper, every dollar buys more flights there — a corner solution. Only if prices were equal would any mix be optimal.

**L02-021** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q15  
A consumer buys bundle A when bundle B was also affordable. What does the theory of revealed preference conclude?

- ▫️ The consumer is indifferent between them
- ✅ A is revealed preferred to B
- ▫️ B is revealed preferred to A
- ▫️ Nothing, because preferences cannot be observed

> If you could have bought B but chose A, you have shown that you prefer A (at least weakly). Revealed preference builds indifference maps from observed choices instead of asking people about their utility.

**L02-022** · one answer · difficulty 3 · exam Q15  
In period 1 a consumer buys A when B is affordable. When would buying B in period 2 violate the Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference (WARP)?

- ▫️ If the consumer's income rises in period 2
- ▫️ If B is cheaper in period 2 than in period 1
- ▫️ If A is no longer affordable in period 2
- ✅ If A is still affordable in period 2

> WARP says that if A is revealed preferred to B, then B must never be chosen when A is also affordable. Choosing B when A is out of reach is consistent; choosing B while A is still affordable contradicts the earlier choice.

## Level 3 · Demand Unpacked — Income & substitution effects, market demand
Exam questions 17–24 · 23 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- Price-consumption curve (PCC): optimal bundles as Px changes → read off the individual demand curve.
- Income-consumption curve (ICC): optimal bundles as income changes → Engel curves (necessity 0 < E < 1, luxury E > 1, inferior E < 0).
- A price change = substitution effect (always opposite to price) + income effect (depends on normal vs inferior).
- Giffen good: inferior AND the income effect outweighs the substitution effect, so demand slopes up. Very rare.
- Market demand = horizontal sum of individual demands. Network effects make demand more elastic (bandwagon) or less elastic (snob).

**L03-001** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q17  
How is an individual demand curve derived from indifference curves?

- ▫️ Plot the marginal utility of X against the quantity of X
- ▫️ Change income with prices fixed and plot income against the chosen X
- ▫️ Add up the quantities chosen by all consumers at each price of the good
- ✅ Vary Px, find each tangency (the PCC) and plot Px against the chosen X

> Each price of X gives a new budget line and a new optimum. Linking these optima traces the PCC; plotting each price against the quantity chosen gives the demand curve. Varying income instead traces the ICC and Engel curve.

**L03-003** · one answer · difficulty 1 · exam Q18

- ▫️ The quantity demanded at each price, with income and other prices held fixed
- ▫️ How spending on a good changes as its price falls
- ▫️ The combinations of two goods that give equal utility

> Engel curves come from the income-consumption curve: as the budget line shifts out in parallel, each new optimum gives one income–quantity pair.

- ▫️ Because the budget line always shifts outward after the price of a good falls

## Level 4 · The Factory Floor — Production functions & returns to scale
Exam questions 25–32 · 24 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- Production function q = F(K, L): the most output that each input combination can give, with today's technology.
- Short run: at least one input (usually capital) is fixed. Long run: every input can change. The length depends on the industry.
- MP > AP pulls the average up, MP < AP pulls it down, so MP cuts AP at AP's maximum.
- Diminishing marginal returns: with other inputs fixed, extra labour eventually adds less output. Output still rises, just more slowly.
- Isoquant slope = MRTS = MPL/MPK. Returns to scale ask what happens when ALL inputs grow together.

**L04-001** · one answer · difficulty 1 · exam Q25  
What does a production function show?

- ▫️ The combinations of two goods an economy can produce with all its resources
- ▫️ The total revenue a firm earns from each level of output it sells
- ▫️ The cheapest way to produce a given level of output at current input prices
- ✅ The maximum output from each combination of inputs, given the technology

> q = F(K, L) is a technical relationship: it says nothing about prices or costs. Cost minimisation comes later, when input prices are added. The last option describes a production possibilities frontier.

**L04-002** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q25  
What separates the short run from the long run in production theory?

- ✅ Whether at least one input is fixed
- ▫️ Whether the period is shorter or longer than one year
- ▫️ Whether the firm is making a profit or a loss
- ▫️ Whether output prices are fixed or flexible during the period

> The short run is any period in which one or more inputs cannot be changed. The long run is long enough to vary all inputs. There is no fixed calendar length: for a coffee shop it may be weeks, for a power plant a decade.

**L04-003** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q25

- ▫️ Chipmakers have no short run, because they always plan years ahead
- ▫️ Capital is fixed only if the firm is unprofitable

> An input is fixed when it cannot be changed within the planning horizon. As the horizon lengthens past the construction time, capacity becomes variable — that is how the short run turns into the long run.

- ▫️ Because MP is always twice as large as AP

> It is like grades: a new score above your average raises it, a score below lowers it. So while MP is above AP, AP rises; once MP falls below AP, AP falls. The turning point is where they are equal.

## Level 5 · Counting Costs — Economic cost, short & long run
Exam questions 33–40 · 23 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- Economic cost = opportunity cost: it includes implicit costs, such as the owner's time, the firm's own building and the owner's capital.
- Sunk costs cannot be recovered, so they must not affect forward-looking decisions.
- TC = FC + VC. MC = ΔTC/Δq = ΔVC/Δq. MC cuts AVC and ATC at their minimum points.
- Cost minimisation: MRTS = w/r, or MPL/w = MPK/r — the last dollar spent on each input yields the same output.
- LAC is the envelope of SAC curves. Economies of scope: joint production is cheaper. Learning curve: cost falls with cumulative output.

- ✅ It includes opportunity costs, even when no money changes hands

> Accountants record explicit outlays and depreciation for financial statements. Economists ask what resources could earn in their best alternative use, so implicit costs such as forgone rent or the owner's forgone salary count too.

## Level 6 · Price Takers — Perfect competition
Exam questions 41–48 · 23 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- Three conditions: price taking, a homogeneous product and free entry and exit.
- Every firm maximises profit at MR = MC. For a price taker MR = P, so it produces where P = MC.
- In the short run, shut down only if P < AVCmin. Fixed costs are paid either way, so they do not matter for this choice.
- Firm supply = the MC curve above AVCmin. Producer surplus = profit + fixed cost = TR − VC.
- In the long run, entry and exit drive economic profit to zero: P = MC = LACmin.

- ▫️ Firms compete mainly through advertising

> Price taking, product homogeneity and free entry/exit are the three conditions. Zero ECONOMIC profit is a long-run outcome, not a condition, and accounting profit is usually positive. Heavy advertising points to differentiated products.

## Level 7 · Hands on the Market — Price controls, taxes, subsidies, tariffs
Exam questions 49–56 · 25 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- Efficiency: a competitive market maximises total surplus (CS + PS). Deadweight loss is surplus that nobody gets.
- Price ceiling: consumers gain A and lose B, producers lose A and C, so the deadweight loss is B + C.
- Price supports and quotas help producers, but at a far higher cost to society than handing them the same money in cash.
- Tariff: consumers lose A + B + C + D, producers gain A, the state collects D, so the deadweight loss is B + C. A quota whose rents go abroad also loses D.
- Tax incidence depends on elasticities, not on who pays the tax office: buyers bear Es/(Es − Ed) of the tax.

**L07-001** · one answer · difficulty 1 · exam Q49

## Level 8 · One Seller, One Buyer — Monopoly & monopsony
Exam questions 57–64 · 22 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- MR = P·(1 + 1/Ed). MR is below price, and it is positive only where demand is elastic.
- A monopolist sets MR = MC, then charges the highest price the demand curve allows at that output. It never operates on the inelastic part.
- Lerner index L = (P − MC)/P = −1/Ed: the more elastic the firm's demand, the smaller its markup.
- Sources of market power: elasticity of market demand, number of firms, how firms interact. Barriers: patents, licences, scale economies.
- Monopsony: marginal expenditure lies above average expenditure, so the buyer purchases less and pays less than under competition.

**L08-001** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q57  
Where does MR = P·(1 + 1/Ed) come from?

- ▫️ MR = P·Q divided by the elasticity of demand
- ✅ MR = P + Q·(ΔP/ΔQ), and Q·(ΔP/ΔQ) = P/Ed
- ▫️ MR equals price times the percentage change in quantity
- ▫️ It is an approximation that only holds for linear demand

> Selling one more unit brings in P but lowers the price on all units sold, by Q·ΔP/ΔQ. Writing that term as P·(Q/P)(ΔP/ΔQ) = P/Ed gives the formula, valid for any demand curve.

- ▫️ Because consumers refuse to buy when demand is inelastic

> With |Ed| < 1, MR = P(1 + 1/Ed) is negative. Producing less raises revenue and saves cost, so profit rises. The monopolist moves up the demand curve until MR = MC > 0, which is only possible where demand is elastic.

## Level 9 · Pricing Power — Price discrimination, two-part tariffs, bundling
Exam questions 65–72 · 22 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- A single price leaves surplus on the table: buyers who would pay more, and buyers who would pay above MC but below the price.
- First degree: charge each buyer their reservation price. Output rises to the competitive level, and the firm captures all surplus.
- Second degree: different prices for different quantities (block pricing). Buyers sort themselves.
- Third degree: separate groups, MR₁ = MR₂ = MC, higher price where demand is less elastic.
- Two-part tariff: entry fee + usage price. Bundling pays when reservation prices are negatively correlated. Tying meters usage.

**L09-001** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q65  
Why does charging one uniform price leave a monopolist's potential profit unrealised?

- ▫️ Because a single price maximises consumer surplus, not profit
- ▫️ Because a single price is always below marginal cost
- ▫️ Because buyers who pay the single price resell the good to others at a higher price
- ✅ It leaves both consumer surplus and deadweight loss uncaptured

> The area above the price under demand is consumer surplus the firm does not collect. The deadweight loss triangle is profitable trade that does not happen. Price discrimination tries to capture both.

**L09-002** · one answer · difficulty 1 · exam Q66  
What is first-degree (perfect) price discrimination?

- ▫️ Charging a fixed fee plus a price per unit
- ▫️ Charging lower prices for larger quantities
- ▫️ Charging different prices to different groups, such as students
- ✅ Charging each customer their own reservation price

> Each unit is sold at the most that buyer is willing to pay. The other options describe second-degree pricing, third-degree pricing and a two-part tariff.

- ▫️ Output falls below the monopoly level and consumer surplus rises
- ▫️ Output stays at the monopoly level, but price is lower
- ✅ Output becomes efficient, but the firm takes all the surplus

> The demand curve becomes the firm's MR curve, because selling an extra unit no longer lowers the price of other units. The firm sells until the reservation price equals MC — the efficient quantity — but consumers keep no surplus.

## Level 10 · The Few — Monopolistic competition & oligopoly
Exam questions 73–80 · 22 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- Monopolistic competition: many firms, differentiated products, free entry. In the long run demand is tangent to LAC: zero profit, but P > MC.
- Excess capacity: output stays below the minimum of LAC — the price of product variety.
- Cournot: firms choose quantities, each taking the rival's output as given. The Nash equilibrium lies between competition and monopoly.
- Bertrand with identical goods: price falls to MC even with two firms. Stackelberg: the firm that commits first wins.
- Kinked demand explains sticky prices. A dominant firm prices off residual demand. Cartels need inelastic demand, inelastic outside supply and discipline.

**L10-001** · select all · difficulty 1 · exam Q73

- ▫️ High barriers that keep new firms out

> Each firm has a little market power from its brand or variety (a downward-sloping demand curve), but free entry eliminates long-run profit. Identical products mean perfect competition; high barriers point to oligopoly or monopoly.

## Level 11 · Game On — Game theory, strategy & auctions
Exam questions 81–88 · 27 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- Dominant strategy: best whatever the rival does. Nash equilibrium: each does its best, given what the other actually does.
- Every dominant-strategy equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium, but not every Nash equilibrium uses dominant strategies.
- Prisoners' dilemma: individually rational choices lead to a worse result for both. Repetition (tit-for-tat) can sustain cooperation.
- Sequential games are solved by backward induction. Only credible threats count, and irreversible commitments can deter entry.
- Auctions: English, Dutch, first-price and second-price sealed-bid. In common-value auctions, beware the winner's curse.

**L11-001** · one answer · difficulty 1 · exam Q81

- ✅ The best choice whatever the rival does
- ▫️ A strategy that is best only if the rival cooperates
- ▫️ A strategy that gives the highest possible payoff in the game
- ▫️ The strategy chosen by the larger firm in an oligopoly

> A dominant strategy does not depend on guessing the rival's choice. It need not give the highest payoff in the game — in a prisoners' dilemma both players have one and still end up badly.

**L11-002** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q81  
What is a Nash equilibrium?

- ▫️ A cooperative agreement enforced by a contract
- ▫️ An outcome that maximises the sum of all players' payoffs
- ▫️ An outcome in which every player has a dominant strategy
- ✅ Each player best-responds to the others

> In a Nash equilibrium no player can gain by changing strategy alone. It does not require dominant strategies, and it need not be efficient.

**L11-003** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q81

- ▫️ Because dominant strategies exist only in sequential games
- ✅ A dominant strategy is a best response to every choice
- ▫️ Because Nash equilibria always involve cooperation

> If your strategy is best against everything, it is certainly best against the rival's equilibrium choice. A Nash strategy only has to be best against what the rival actually does, so it may not be dominant.

## Level 12 · Hired Inputs — Labour & factor markets
Exam questions 89–96 · 19 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- Input demand is derived from demand for the output. A firm hires until the marginal revenue product equals the input price.
- MRP_L = MP_L × MR. With competitive output MR = P, so MRP = VMP = MP × P. With output monopoly, MRP < VMP.
- Industry input demand is steeper than the sum of firms' MRPs, because the output price falls when all firms expand.
- Labour supply bends backward if the income effect of a higher wage outweighs the substitution effect.
- Economic rent = pay above what is needed to keep the input in use. Unions, monopsony and bilateral monopoly change wages.

## Level 13 · Time Is Money — Capital, interest & risk
Exam questions 97–104 · 25 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- Stocks are measured at a point in time (machines, capacity); flows per period (investment, depreciation, revenue).

## Level 14 · The Big Box — General equilibrium & efficiency
Exam questions 105–111 · 23 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- Partial equilibrium looks at one market; general equilibrium follows the feedback between related markets.
- Edgeworth box: if MRS_A ≠ MRS_B, both can gain by trading. The contract curve is the set of allocations where MRS_A = MRS_B.
- First welfare theorem: a competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient. Efficiency says nothing about fairness.
- Production efficiency: equal MRTS across industries, which traces the PPF. Output efficiency: MRS = MRT = MCx/MCy.
- Trade lets a country consume outside its PPF, even if a partner has an absolute advantage in everything.

**L14-001** · one answer · difficulty 1 · exam Q105

- ▫️ It ignores prices and focuses on quantities alone
- ▫️ It applies only to monopolies and oligopolies
- ✅ It tracks feedback between markets
- ▫️ It studies one market while holding all other markets fixed

> Partial equilibrium looks at one market and treats others as given. General equilibrium determines prices and quantities in several markets at once, tracing how a change in one feeds into others and back.

**L14-002** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q105

- ▫️ No other markets change, because only ethanol is taxed

> Substitutes (domestic ethanol, gasoline) and inputs (corn) are affected, and through corn, livestock feed and food prices. These changes can feed back into the ethanol market, so the full effect differs from the single-market estimate.

**L14-003** · one answer · difficulty 3 · exam Q105

- ▫️ Because partial equilibrium ignores the demand curve
- ▫️ Because partial equilibrium always overstates price changes
- ▫️ Because general equilibrium effects are too small to matter
- ✅ Feedback from related markets changes the result

> In Pindyck's example of a tax on one good, rising demand for a substitute raises its price, which shifts demand back to the taxed good. The final price change can be larger or smaller than the one-market analysis predicts.

## Level 15 · When Markets Fail — Externalities & public goods
Exam questions 112–120 · 28 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- Four sources of market failure: market power, incomplete information, externalities, public goods.
- Negative externality: MSC = MC + MEC, so the market overproduces. Positive externality: MSB = D + MEB, so it underproduces.
- Fixes: a Pigouvian fee equal to MEC at the efficient output, standards, tradable permits, or Coase-style bargaining.
- Common property (rival, non-excludable) is overused: the tragedy of the commons.
- Public goods (non-rival, non-excludable) invite free riders. The efficient level is where the VERTICAL sum of marginal benefits equals MC.

**L15-001** · select all · difficulty 1 · exam Q112

> Each breaks a condition of the First Welfare Theorem: market power drives P above MC, poor information distorts choices, externalities leave costs or benefits out of prices, and public goods cannot be charged for. High prices alone are not a market failure.

> The subsidy raises the private return to equal the social return, so private decision-makers choose the efficient quantity. Patents, public research funding and vaccination subsidies are examples.

**L15-020** · one answer · difficulty 1 · exam Q118  
What is a common property resource?

- ▫️ A good that is non-rival and non-excludable
- ✅ A rival resource that nobody can be kept from using
- ▫️ A resource whose use is controlled by a market price

> Ocean fisheries and busy shipping routes are rival (my catch reduces yours) but non-excludable (no one can easily be kept out). Non-rival, non-excludable goods are public goods.

**L15-021** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q118  
Why does free access to a fishery lead to overfishing?

- ▫️ Because fishers prefer to catch fewer fish than is efficient

> Each user fishes until private cost equals the benefit, ignoring that each extra catch depletes the stock for others. Too much fishing occurs compared with the social optimum, where marginal social cost equals marginal benefit.

**L15-022** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q118

- ▫️ By subsidising extra boats to raise competition

**L15-023** · one answer · difficulty 1 · exam Q119  
Which two properties define a pure public good?

- ▫️ Rival and excludable
- ▫️ Non-rival and excludable
- ✅ Non-rival and non-excludable
- ▫️ Rival and non-excludable

> Non-rival: one person's use does not reduce what others get. Non-excludable: nobody can be kept from enjoying it. Rival + non-excludable is common property; non-rival + excludable is a club good (pay-TV).

**L15-024** · select all · difficulty 2 · exam Q119

**L15-025** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q119

**L15-026** · one answer · difficulty 1 · exam Q120

**L15-027** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q120  
Why are individual demand curves summed VERTICALLY to find the efficient level of a public good?

- ✅ Everyone consumes the same quantity, so their marginal benefits add up
- ▫️ Because vertical summation gives the market price of the good
- ▫️ Because each unit can only be consumed by one person
- ▫️ Because public goods are always provided by the state

> For a private good, people consume different quantities at one price, so we add quantities (horizontally). For a public good, everyone gets the same quantity, so society's marginal benefit at each quantity is the sum of individual marginal benefits.

**L15-028** · one answer · difficulty 2 · exam Q120

## Exam questions — oral practice cards

**1. Market mechanism, supply shocks and shortages**
- Excess demand bids the price up, excess supply pushes it down, until Qd = Qs
- A supply disruption (strike, war) shifts supply left: price rises, quantity falls
- That excess demand is temporary, because the price is free to rise and clear the market
- A binding price ceiling blocks the price rise, so the shortage persists and rationing appears
- ✏️ Draw: Two panels: supply shifting left with a new higher equilibrium; a ceiling below P₀ with the shortage Q₂ − Q₁ (📊 ceiling)

**2. Movement along vs shift of the supply curve**
- Movement along: caused only by a change in the good's own price
- Shift: caused by costs, technology, capacity, input prices, number of sellers, policy
- Oil example — movement: higher world price makes shale firms drill more wells
- Oil example — shift: cheaper fracking technology (right) or OPEC+ quota cuts (left)
- ✏️ Draw: One supply curve with an arrow along it; a second panel with S shifting to S′

**3. Price elasticity, point vs arc, slope, maximum spending**
- Ep = % change in Q ÷ % change in P; it has no units
- Point elasticity uses one point; arc elasticity uses midpoints so the direction does not matter
- On a straight line the slope is constant but P/Q changes, so elasticity changes along the line
- Spending P·Q peaks at the midpoint, where |E| = 1: elastic above, inelastic below
- 🧮 `Ep = (ΔQ/ΔP)·(P/Q)`
- 🧮 `Arc: E = (ΔQ/ΔP)·(P̄/Q̄)`
- 🧮 `Linear demand Q = a − bP: E = −bP/Q`
- ✏️ Draw: Straight demand line marked "elastic" above the midpoint, "|E| = 1" at the midpoint, "inelastic" below

**4. Income and cross-price elasticity (EV example)**
- Income elasticity: < 0 inferior, 0–1 necessity, > 1 luxury
- Cross-price elasticity: > 0 substitutes, < 0 complements, 0 unrelated
- +0.8 between imported EVs and gasoline cars: substitutes — rivals' price moves shift your sales
- +2.2 for luxury EVs: sales grow faster than income and swing with the business cycle; target rich, fast-growing markets
- 🧮 `E_I = (ΔQ/Q)/(ΔI/I)`
- 🧮 `E_XY = (ΔQx/Qx)/(ΔPy/Py)`

**5. Short-run vs long-run demand elasticity: gasoline vs aircraft**
- Nondurables (gasoline): demand is more elastic in the long run — people change cars, homes, habits
- Durables (cars, aircraft): demand is more elastic in the short run — buyers can postpone replacement
- In the long run the stock of durables must be maintained, so purchases recover
- Gasoline has greater long-run elasticity; aircraft orders swing sharply in the short run
- ✏️ Draw: Two demand curves for each good: steep SR and flat LR for gasoline; the reverse for aircraft

**6. Long-run supply elasticity and secondary supply (scrap)**
- Short run: capacity (mines, smelters, land) is fixed, so supply is inelastic
- Long run: new capacity comes on stream, so supply is much more elastic
- Secondary supply (scrap) is more elastic in the short run than in the long run: high prices pull scrap out, but the stock runs down
- Recycling raises the short-run elasticity of total supply (Pindyck's copper table)
- ✏️ Draw: Steep short-run supply and flatter long-run supply through the same point

**7. Copper demand shift (calculation task)** · 🧮 calculation task
- Method: set QD = QS for the original equilibrium, then scale demand by 1.2 and solve again
- "20% more at every price" multiplies the whole demand function: both intercept and slope
- Original: 32 − 0.20P = 18 + 0.12P gives P = 43.75, Q = 23.25
- New: 38.4 − 0.24P = 18 + 0.12P gives P ≈ 56.67, Q = 24.8 (price +30%, quantity +7%)
- Price rises much more than quantity because supply is relatively inelastic
- 🧮 `Q′D = 1.2·(32 − 0.20P) = 38.4 − 0.24P`
- ✏️ Draw: Supply line with demand shifting right; label old and new equilibrium

**8. Price ceiling on an essential import**
- Below equilibrium, importers bring in less while buyers want more: a shortage
- Gainers: buyers who still get the good at the low price
- Losers: rationed-out buyers, importers and foreign suppliers; deadweight loss B + C
- Price no longer rations, so queues, coupons, favouritism or black markets do
- ✏️ Draw: Ceiling below P₀; mark Q₁ (supplied), Q₂ (demanded), areas A, B, C (📊 ceiling)

**9. Preference assumptions; indifference curves cannot cross**
- Completeness, transitivity, more is better, diminishing MRS (convexity)
- Utility is ordinal: it only ranks bundles
- If two curves crossed, transitivity would make two bundles equally good even though one has more of both goods — contradicting "more is better"
- ✏️ Draw: Two crossing indifference curves with points A, B, D to show the contradiction

**10. Marginal rate of substitution**
- MRS of X for Y: the most Y a consumer gives up for one more X and stays equally well off
- It is the absolute slope of the indifference curve
- Diminishing MRS: the more X you have, the less Y you give up for more X — convex curves
- 🧮 `MRS = −ΔY/ΔX = MUx/MUy`
- ✏️ Draw: Convex indifference curve with two slope triangles getting flatter to the right (📊 consumerChoice)

**11. Special preferences: substitutes, complements, bads, neutral goods**
- Perfect substitutes: straight lines, constant MRS; optimum usually a corner
- Perfect complements (Leontief): L-shapes; optimum at the kink; no substitution effect
- A bad (pollution): upward-sloping curves; consumer chooses zero of it if possible
- Neutral good X: horizontal lines (MRS = 0); all income goes to Y
- 🧮 `Substitutes U = aX + bY: MRS = a/b`
- 🧮 `Complements U = min(aX, bY): MRS undefined at the kink`
- ✏️ Draw: Four small panels with the four shapes and the optimum on each

**12. Budget constraint and how it shifts**
- Budget line: I = Px·X + Py·Y; intercepts I/Px and I/Py
- Slope = −Px/Py, the market rate of exchange
- 20% inflation in all prices, nominal income fixed: parallel inward shift (slope unchanged)
- 10% tariff on X only: rotates inward around the Y-intercept, slope −1.1·Px/Py
- 🧮 `Y = I/Py − (Px/Py)·X`
- ✏️ Draw: Original line; a parallel inward line; a line pivoted around the Y-intercept

**13. Utility maximisation: MRS = Px/Py**
- At an interior optimum the budget line is tangent to the highest reachable indifference curve
- Equal marginal principle: the last dollar on each good gives the same utility
- MRS > Px/Py: the consumer values X above its price, so buys more X and less Y until MRS falls
- 🧮 `MRS = MUx/MUy = Px/Py`
- 🧮 `MUx/Px = MUy/Py`
- ✏️ Draw: Budget line tangent to U₂ at A; points B, C on U₁ with arrows toward A (📊 consumerChoice)

**14. Corner solutions**
- A corner solution: the consumer buys none of one good
- It arises when MRS ≠ Px/Py even at the edge of the budget line (e.g. perfect substitutes)
- Airlines as perfect substitutes (MRS = 1): all flights go to the cheaper airline; any mix only if prices are equal
- 🧮 `Corner at X = 0 when MRS < Px/Py for every bundle`
- ✏️ Draw: Straight-line indifference curves meeting the budget line at an axis

**15. Revealed preference and WARP**
- If A is chosen when B is affordable, A is revealed preferred to B
- Revealed preference builds preferences from observed choices
- WARP: if A is revealed preferred to B, B must never be chosen when A is also affordable
- Choosing B later is consistent only if A has become unaffordable
- ✏️ Draw: Two budget lines: A chosen on the first, B on the second; shade the revealed-preferred region

**16. Laspeyres vs Paasche and CPI bias**
- Laspeyres uses base-period quantities: it overstates the cost-of-living increase
- Paasche uses current-period quantities: it understates it
- Bias comes from substitution toward goods that became relatively cheaper
- Other CPI biases: quality changes and new goods added late; chain-weighting reduces the bias
- 🧮 `LI = Σ P₁Q₀ / Σ P₀Q₀`
- 🧮 `PI = Σ P₁Q₁ / Σ P₀Q₁`

**17. Price-consumption curve and individual demand**
- Vary Px with income and Py fixed; link the tangencies to form the PCC
- Plot each Px against the X chosen: the individual demand curve
- With Y as "all other goods": downward PCC = elastic, horizontal = unit elastic, upward = inelastic demand
- ✏️ Draw: Upper panel: budget lines pivoting with three tangencies; lower panel: the derived demand curve

**18. Income-consumption curve and Engel curves**
- Shift the budget line out in parallel; link the tangencies to form the ICC
- Engel curve: quantity of X at each income level
- Necessity 0 < E_I < 1 (budget share falls), luxury E_I > 1 (share rises), inferior E_I < 0 (Engel curve bends back)
- 🧮 `E_I = (ΔQ/Q)/(ΔI/I)`
- ✏️ Draw: ICC panel above, three Engel curves below: necessity, luxury, inferior

**19. Hicksian decomposition: substitution and income effects**
- Draw a hypothetical budget line with the new slope, tangent to the original indifference curve
- Substitution effect: move along U₁ to that tangency — always opposite to the price change
- Income effect: move from the hypothetical line to the new optimum; same direction for a normal good
- Convex indifference curves guarantee the negative substitution effect
- ✏️ Draw: Original and pivoted budget lines, the dashed hypothetical line, points A → B (SE) → C (IE)

**20. Inferior vs Giffen goods**
- Inferior: demand falls as income rises (negative income effect)
- Giffen: inferior AND the income effect outweighs the substitution effect, so demand slopes up
- Every Giffen good is inferior; most inferior goods are not Giffen
- Plausible only for staples taking a large budget share of very poor households (Jensen & Miller, rice in China)
- ✏️ Draw: Decomposition where the income effect arrow is longer and points opposite to the substitution effect

**21. Horizontal summation and market demand**
- Market demand: add quantities demanded by all buyers at each price
- New foreign buyers shift market demand to the right
- Kinks appear where a new buyer's choke price is reached and their demand is added
- ✏️ Draw: Two individual demands and their horizontal sum with a kink

**22. Consumer surplus; tariffs and transport costs**
- Consumer surplus: willingness to pay minus the price paid, summed over units
- Graphically: area under demand and above price
- A tariff or higher transport cost raises price: CS falls by a trapezoid (rectangle on units still bought + triangle on units given up)
- 🧮 `CS ≈ ½·(Pmax − P)·Q for linear demand`
- ✏️ Draw: Demand with CS triangle; a higher price line showing the lost trapezoid

**23. Network externalities: bandwagon and snob effects**
- Bandwagon (positive): more users make the good more valuable; a price cut draws users, which shifts demand out again — more elastic
- Snob (negative): more owners reduce exclusivity; a price cut's effect is partly offset — less elastic
- Examples: messaging and payment platforms (bandwagon); luxury watches and limited editions (snob)
- ✏️ Draw: Demand curves for 20k, 40k, 60k users; market demand flatter (bandwagon) or steeper (snob)

**24. Speculative demand**
- Demand driven by expected future price rises, not by the good's direct use
- Rising prices can raise expected prices, shifting demand right: a feedback loop
- The law of demand still holds for utility-based demand; expectations are a shift factor
- Examples: housing booms, commodity hoarding — bubbles end when expectations turn
- ✏️ Draw: Demand shifting right repeatedly as price rises

**25. Production function; short run vs long run**
- Production function: maximum output from each input combination with given technology
- Short run: at least one input fixed (usually capital); long run: all inputs variable
- An asset is fixed when it cannot be changed within the planning horizon (fab construction takes years)
- As the horizon lengthens, fixed inputs become variable
- 🧮 `q = F(K, L)`

**26. TP, AP and MP; why MP cuts AP at its maximum**
- AP = q/L; MP = Δq/ΔL
- MP above AP pulls AP up; MP below AP pulls it down, so MP = AP at AP's maximum
- Graph: AP = slope of a ray from the origin to TP; MP = slope of TP
- TP is at its maximum where MP = 0
- 🧮 `dAP/dL = (MP − AP)/L`
- ✏️ Draw: TP curve above; AP and MP below, with MP crossing AP at AP's peak

**27. Diminishing marginal returns**
- With other inputs fixed, the marginal product of an input eventually falls
- Output still rises as long as MP > 0 — it just rises more slowly
- Better technology shifts TP upward and delays diminishing returns (Malthus was wrong)
- Applies to one input at a time, not to scaling all inputs
- ✏️ Draw: TP curve shifting up from O₁ to O₂ to O₃ with technological progress

**28. Isoquants and isoquant maps**
- Isoquant: all input combinations giving the same output
- Downward sloping (both MPs positive), convex (diminishing MRTS), never crossing
- An upward-sloping section means one input has a negative MP — an uneconomic region
- ✏️ Draw: Isoquant map with q₁ < q₂ < q₃; a bent-back section outside the ridge lines

**29. MRTS = MPL/MPK**
- MRTS: how much capital can be replaced by one more unit of labour at constant output
- Output constant along the isoquant gives the ratio of marginal products
- MRTS diminishes: more labour lowers MPL and raises MPK
- 🧮 `MPL·ΔL + MPK·ΔK = 0`
- 🧮 `MRTS = −ΔK/ΔL = MPL/MPK`
- ✏️ Draw: Convex isoquant with flatter slope triangles moving right

**30. Linear, Leontief and Cobb-Douglas technologies**
- Linear (perfect substitutes): straight isoquants, elasticity of substitution σ = ∞
- Fixed proportions (Leontief): L-shaped isoquants, σ = 0
- Cobb-Douglas: smooth convex isoquants, σ = 1
- High σ makes a firm resilient to shocks in one input; Leontief firms cannot switch
- 🧮 `q = aL + bK;  q = min(aL, bK);  q = A·K^α·L^β`
- 🧮 `σ = %Δ(K/L) / %ΔMRTS`
- ✏️ Draw: Three isoquant panels side by side

**31. Returns to scale vs diminishing returns**
- Increasing, constant, decreasing RTS: output rises by more, the same or less than the proportional rise in all inputs
- Diminishing returns concern one input; returns to scale concern all inputs together
- Example q = K^0.6·L^0.6: each MP diminishes (0.6 < 1), but 0.6 + 0.6 = 1.2 > 1 gives IRS
- 🧮 `F(tK, tL) = t^(α+β)·F(K, L)`
- ✏️ Draw: Isoquants for q = 10, 20, 30 getting closer together along a ray (IRS)

**32. Economies and diseconomies of scale in global industries**
- Sources: huge indivisible fixed costs (fabs, design, certification), specialisation, volume purchasing
- Chips, cars, aerospace: few producers worldwide, because minimum efficient scale is huge
- Diseconomies: layers of hierarchy, slow decisions, weak incentives, information loss
- Scale economies concern all inputs; external input-price effects are a separate idea
- ✏️ Draw: U-shaped LAC with economies, minimum efficient scale and diseconomies marked

**33. Accounting, economic and opportunity cost**
- Accounting cost: explicit outlays and depreciation
- Economic cost = opportunity cost: includes implicit costs
- A firm's own building: no rent in the books, but forgone rental income is an economic cost
- Owner's equity: forgone return elsewhere is a cost, so accounting profit can hide an economic loss
- 🧮 `Economic profit = revenue − explicit costs − implicit costs`

**34. Sunk costs**
- Sunk cost: already spent and unrecoverable
- Ignore sunk costs: only future costs and benefits matter for the decision
- Example: spent $50m, need $20m more to earn $30m — finish it
- Not sunk: assets with resale value, avoidable future costs

**35. Short-run cost curves**
- TC = FC + VC; ATC = AFC + AVC
- MC = ΔVC/Δq, so fixed cost does not affect MC
- MC cuts AVC and ATC at their minimum points; AFC falls continuously
- 🧮 `MC = ΔTC/Δq = ΔVC/Δq = w/MPL`
- ✏️ Draw: U-shaped MC, ATC and AVC with MC crossing both at their minimums (📊 firmCosts)

**36. Isocost lines and cost minimisation**
- Isocost: C = wL + rK, slope −w/r
- Lowest cost for a given output: isocost tangent to the isoquant
- If MPL/w > MPK/r, use more labour and less capital
- 🧮 `MRTS = MPL/MPK = w/r`
- 🧮 `MPL/w = MPK/r`
- ✏️ Draw: Isoquant with three isocost lines; tangency at the cheapest one

**37. Expansion path after a wage increase**
- Expansion path: cost-minimising input mixes for each output level
- If w doubles with r fixed, isocosts get steeper: the firm substitutes capital for labour
- K/L rises; the expansion path rotates toward the capital axis
- How far it rotates depends on the elasticity of substitution
- ✏️ Draw: Isoquants with old and new tangencies; old and new expansion paths from the origin

**38. LAC as the envelope of SAC curves**
- For each output, the long-run choice is the plant with the lowest SAC
- LAC touches each SAC once and never lies above any SAC
- With economies of scale the tangency is left of the SAC minimum; with diseconomies to its right
- Only at the bottom of LAC does tangency fall on an SAC's minimum
- ✏️ Draw: Several SAC curves with LAC as their lower envelope

**39. Economies of scope**
- Joint production of two products costs less than producing them separately
- Different from scale (volume of one product) and from learning
- Sources: shared networks, hubs, IT, brands, R&D (logistics, consumer electronics)
- SC > 0 means economies of scope; SC < 0 diseconomies
- 🧮 `SC = [C(q₁) + C(q₂) − C(q₁, q₂)] / C(q₁, q₂)`

**40. Learning curve**
- Cost per unit falls as cumulative output rises (workers, managers and engineers learn)
- Learning shifts cost curves down over time; scale moves along LAC with the output rate
- 80% curve: each doubling of cumulative output cuts labour hours per unit to 80%
- Rationale for aggressive early pricing (aircraft, chips)
- 🧮 `L = A + B·N^(−β)`
- ✏️ Draw: Labour hours per unit falling with cumulative output; LAC₁ shifting down to LAC₂

**41. Perfect competition: conditions and horizontal firm demand**
- Price taking, homogeneous product, free entry and exit
- The firm's output is tiny, and buyers can switch to identical goods
- So firm demand is perfectly elastic at the market price, while market demand slopes down
- ✏️ Draw: Two panels: market S and D setting P; the firm's horizontal d = MR = P

**42. MR = MC and P = MC**
- Profit π(q) = R(q) − C(q) peaks where dπ/dq = MR − MC = 0
- If MR > MC, expand; if MR < MC, contract
- For a price taker MR = P, so P = MC; MC must be rising (second-order condition)
- 🧮 `dπ/dq = MR − MC = 0`
- ✏️ Draw: Firm diagram: P line crossing rising MC at q*

**43. Short-run losses and the shutdown rule**
- Produce at a loss if P ≥ AVCmin: revenue covers variable cost and part of fixed cost
- Shut down if P < AVCmin
- Fixed costs are paid either way, so they do not affect the decision
- Long-run exit if P < ATC persists
- 🧮 `Shut down if P < AVCmin  (equivalently R < VC)`
- ✏️ Draw: Cost curves with prices above ATCmin, between AVCmin and ATCmin, and below AVCmin (📊 firmCosts)

**44. The firm's short-run supply curve**
- For each price, the firm produces where P = MC
- Below AVCmin it produces nothing
- So supply = MC curve above AVCmin
- A higher variable input price shifts MC up and supply left; fixed costs do not shift it
- ✏️ Draw: The bold part of MC above the shutdown point (📊 firmCosts)

**45. Short-run market supply**
- With constant input prices: horizontal sum of firms' supply curves
- If input prices rise as the whole industry expands, each MC shifts up
- So industry supply is steeper than the simple horizontal sum
- ✏️ Draw: Three firms' MC curves and their horizontal sum

**46. Producer surplus vs profit**
- Producer surplus: sum over units of (P − MC); area above supply, below price
- PS = profit + FC = TR − VC
- In the long run (no fixed costs) PS and profit coincide
- Market PS: area above market supply and below price
- 🧮 `PS = R − VC;  π = R − VC − FC`
- ✏️ Draw: Firm panel and market panel with PS shaded

**47. Long-run competitive equilibrium**
- Profits attract entry, losses cause exit, shifting market supply
- Long run: zero economic profit, P = MC = LACmin
- Zero economic profit = normal return; accounting profit is positive
- Owners stay because they earn their opportunity cost
- 🧮 `P = MC = LACmin`
- ✏️ Draw: Market supply shifting right to S′ and the firm at the bottom of LAC

**48. Long-run industry supply: constant, increasing, decreasing cost**
- Constant-cost: input prices unchanged as the industry grows — horizontal supply at LACmin
- Increasing-cost: scarce inputs get dearer — upward-sloping supply
- Decreasing-cost: suppliers' scale economies cut input prices — downward-sloping supply
- External (pecuniary) economies and diseconomies decide which case applies
- ✏️ Draw: Demand shifting right with three different long-run supply curves

**49. Efficiency and the deadweight loss of a price ceiling**
- Efficiency: competitive equilibrium maximises CS + PS
- Under a ceiling: ΔCS = A − B, ΔPS = −A − C, deadweight loss = B + C
- DWL is largest with elastic supply and inelastic demand; zero with perfectly inelastic supply
- With very inelastic demand, consumers as a group can lose (B > A)
- 🧮 `DWL = B + C`
- ✏️ Draw: Ceiling diagram with areas A, B, C (📊 ceiling)

**50. Price floors and minimum wages**
- A floor above equilibrium creates excess supply (unemployment for a minimum wage)
- Workers who keep jobs gain; employers and priced-out workers lose
- Deadweight loss on trades that no longer happen
- If producers make more than they can sell, the cost of unsold output can wipe out their gain
- ✏️ Draw: Floor above P₀ with Qd, Qs and the excess supply; triangles B, C

**51. Price supports vs direct subsidies**
- Support: the state buys the surplus (Q₂ − Q₁) at Ps
- Consumers lose, producers gain, taxpayers pay for unwanted output
- Total cost to consumers and taxpayers far exceeds producers' gain
- A direct cash transfer of the same gain is much cheaper and creates no waste
- 🧮 `Cost to state = Ps·(Q₂ − Q₁)`
- ✏️ Draw: Support price above P₀; consumer loss A + B, producer gain A + B + D, state purchases

**52. Production quotas and licences (taxi medallions)**
- A cap on quantity raises price above MC
- Consumers lose; incumbents with licences gain the rents
- Lost trades are a deadweight loss
- Tradable licences capture the rent in their price (New York medallions)
- ✏️ Draw: Vertical supply cap at Q₁ below Q₀; areas A (transfer), B + C (DWL)

**53. Import tariff in a small importing country**
- Tariff raises the domestic price from Pw to Pw + t
- Consumers lose A + B + C + D; producers gain A; state revenue D
- Deadweight loss B (production distortion) + C (consumption distortion)
- 🧮 `Revenue D = t·(Q′d − Q′s)`
- ✏️ Draw: World price line, tariff line, areas A, B, D, C (📊 tariff)

**54. Quota vs tariff; quota rents**
- A quota with the same import volume raises the price by the same amount
- If the state auctions licences (or domestic firms hold them), the loss is still B + C
- If foreign exporters get the quota rent (VERs), the country also loses D
- Example: Japan's car export restraints to the US in the 1980s
- ✏️ Draw: Same diagram as the tariff, with D labelled "quota rent" (📊 tariff)

**55. Tax incidence and pass-through**
- A tax opens a wedge Pb − Ps = t
- The less elastic side bears more of the burden
- Statutory incidence does not matter: taxing buyers or sellers gives the same result
- Revenue A + D, deadweight loss B + C
- 🧮 `Buyers' share = Es/(Es − Ed)`
- 🧮 `Sellers' share = −Ed/(Es − Ed)`
- ✏️ Draw: Tax wedge diagram with Pb, Ps, Q₁ and areas A, D, B, C (📊 tax)

**56. Per-unit subsidy**
- A subsidy s creates a wedge Ps − Pb = s
- Output expands past the efficient level: extra units cost more than buyers value them
- Taxpayers' cost exceeds the gain in CS + PS: a deadweight loss
- Benefit split by the same elasticity rule as a tax
- 🧮 `Buyers' share = Es/(Es − Ed)`
- ✏️ Draw: Supply and demand with Ps above and Pb below P₀, quantity Q₁ > Q₀, DWL triangle

**57. MR, price and elasticity; why monopoly avoids inelastic demand**
- Selling one more unit brings in P but lowers the price on all units
- MR is positive only where demand is elastic (|Ed| > 1)
- On the inelastic part MR < 0: cutting output raises revenue and lowers cost
- 🧮 `MR = P + Q·(ΔP/ΔQ) = P·(1 + 1/Ed)`
- ✏️ Draw: Linear demand with MR twice as steep, MR = 0 at the midpoint

**58. Lerner index and the markup rule**
- Lerner index measures monopoly power: 0 under competition
- Profit maximisation gives the inverse-elasticity rule
- Import substitutes make the firm's demand more elastic and shrink its markup
- 🧮 `L = (P − MC)/P = −1/Ed`
- 🧮 `P = MC / (1 + 1/Ed)`

**59. Sources of monopoly power and barriers to entry**
- Elasticity of market demand
- Number of firms in the market
- Interaction among firms
- Barriers: patents, copyrights, licences, scale economies, control of key inputs

**60. Deadweight loss of monopoly and rent-seeking**
- Monopoly output Qm < Qc, price Pm > Pc
- Consumers lose A + B, producer gains A − C, deadweight loss B + C
- Rent-seeking (lobbying to win or keep monopoly) can waste much of A as well
- 🧮 `Deadweight loss = B + C`
- ✏️ Draw: Demand, MR, MC with Pm, Qm, Pc, Qc and areas A, B, C (📊 monopoly)

**61. Price ceiling on a monopolist**
- Below the cap, MR equals the cap price up to the demand curve
- A cap at the competitive price raises output to Qc and removes the deadweight loss
- A cap set too low cuts output and creates a shortage
- In a competitive industry a ceiling always reduces output
- ✏️ Draw: Monopoly diagram with a horizontal cap line and the new kinked MR

**62. Natural monopoly and regulation**
- Subadditive costs: one firm can serve the market more cheaply than several
- Typically LAC keeps falling over the relevant range (grids, water, rail)
- P = MC is efficient but below AC: the firm loses money
- P = AC lets it break even but leaves a small deadweight loss
- ✏️ Draw: Falling AC with MC below it; mark Pm, P = AC and P = MC outcomes

**63. Monopsony: average vs marginal expenditure**
- Monopsony: a single (or dominant) buyer
- AE is the supply curve the buyer faces
- To buy one more unit it must pay more on all units, so ME > AE
- 🧮 `ME = AE + Q·(ΔAE/ΔQ)`
- ✏️ Draw: Upward-sloping AE and a steeper ME above it (📊 monopsony)

**64. Monopsony equilibrium and deadweight loss**
- Buy where MV = ME, then pay the AE (supply) price
- Less is bought at a lower price than under competition
- Buyer gains A − B, sellers lose A + C, deadweight loss B + C
- Regional labour markets with one big employer
- 🧮 `MV = ME`
- ✏️ Draw: MV, AE, ME with Qm, Pm and areas A, B, C (📊 monopsony)

**65. Why a single price leaves profit on the table**
- Some buyers would pay more than the single price (uncaptured consumer surplus)
- Some would buy at prices above MC but below the single price (deadweight loss)
- Price discrimination aims to capture both
- ✏️ Draw: Monopoly diagram highlighting CS above Pm and the DWL triangle

**66. First-degree price discrimination**
- Charge each buyer their reservation price
- Needs knowledge of willingness to pay and no resale
- Demand becomes the MR curve, so output rises to where D = MC
- No deadweight loss, but all consumer surplus goes to the firm
- ✏️ Draw: Demand and MC with the whole area between them shaded as profit

**67. Second-degree price discrimination**
- Different unit prices for different quantities (block pricing, quantity discounts)
- Self-selection: buyers choose their block or plan, revealing their type
- Utilities and telecoms extract surplus without knowing individual demand
- ✏️ Draw: Stepped price schedule P₁, P₂, P₃ over blocks of quantity

**68. Third-degree price discrimination**
- Separate groups (countries, students) with different elasticities
- Allocate output so that MR is equal across markets and equal to MC
- Higher price where demand is less elastic
- 🧮 `MR₁ = MR₂ = MC`
- 🧮 `P₁/P₂ = (1 + 1/E₂)/(1 + 1/E₁)`
- ✏️ Draw: Two market panels plus combined MR_T crossing MC

**69. Intertemporal discrimination vs peak-load pricing**
- Intertemporal: high launch price for eager buyers, lower later (electronics, hardcover then paperback)
- Peak-load: higher prices at peak times because MC is higher when capacity is stretched
- Peak-load pricing is efficient (P ≈ MC in each period), not surplus extraction
- 🧮 `Peak-load: MR = MC in each period`
- ✏️ Draw: Two demand curves (peak, off-peak) with the same rising MC

**70. Two-part tariff**
- Price = entry fee T + usage price P per unit
- Identical consumers: P = MC, T = each consumer's consumer surplus
- Different consumers: P > MC and a lower T, trading off entry fees against usage margins
- Examples: amusement parks, gyms, razors and blades, phone plans
- 🧮 `Profit = n·T + (P − MC)·Q`

**71. Pure and mixed bundling**
- Pure: only the package is sold; mixed: package and items separately
- Bundling pays when reservation prices are negatively correlated
- Mixed bundling beats pure when MC is significant and some buyers value one item below cost
- Examples: software suites, media and streaming licences
- ✏️ Draw: Reservation-price scatter (r₁, r₂) with a negative correlation and the bundle price line

**72. Tying as metering**
- Buyers of the main product must buy the firm's supplies
- Usage intensity reveals willingness to pay, so heavy users pay more
- Examples: printers and cartridges, Xerox paper, IBM punch cards
- Also used to protect quality (franchising) — watched by competition authorities

**73. Monopolistic competition and long-run tangency**
- Many firms, differentiated products, free entry
- Short-run profits attract entry; each firm's demand shifts in
- Long run: demand tangent to LAC — zero profit, P > MC
- 🧮 `Long run: MR = MC and P = LAC`
- ✏️ Draw: Short-run panel with profit; long-run panel with tangency

**74. Excess capacity theorem**
- Tangency is on the falling part of LAC, so output is below minimum efficient scale
- Costs are higher than the minimum, and P > MC
- Trade-off: the inefficiency is the price of variety consumers value
- Small if products are close substitutes (very elastic demand)
- ✏️ Draw: LAC with the tangency point left of its minimum

**75. Cournot duopoly**
- Firms choose quantities simultaneously, each taking the other's output as given
- Reaction function: best output for each rival output
- Nash equilibrium where reaction curves cross
- Output between monopoly and competition; price between too
- 🧮 `Example P = 30 − Q, MC = 0: Q₁ = 15 − Q₂/2;  Q₁ = Q₂ = 10, P = 10`
- ✏️ Draw: Reaction curves crossing at 10, 10 with collusion and competition lines (📊 cournot)

**76. Bertrand price competition**
- Firms choose prices simultaneously; identical products
- Any price above MC can be undercut to win the whole market
- So P = MC even with two firms (Bertrand paradox)
- Resolved by capacity constraints, differentiation or repeated interaction
- 🧮 `Equilibrium: P₁ = P₂ = MC`

**77. Stackelberg leadership**
- The leader commits to output first; the follower responds on its reaction curve
- The leader picks the point on the follower's reaction curve that maximises its own profit
- First-mover advantage: leader produces more and earns more than in Cournot; follower less
- 🧮 `Example: Q₁ = 15, Q₂ = 7.5 (Cournot 10, 10)`
- ✏️ Draw: Stackelberg point on firm 2's reaction curve (📊 cournot)

**78. Kinked demand curve (Sweezy)**
- Rivals match price cuts but not price rises
- Demand is elastic above the kink and inelastic below it
- MR has a vertical gap at Q*
- MC can move within the gap without changing P* or Q*: price rigidity
- Weakness: it does not explain how P* is set
- ✏️ Draw: Kinked demand, broken MR and two MC curves through the gap (📊 kinked)

**79. Dominant firm and competitive fringe**
- Residual demand = market demand − fringe supply
- The dominant firm sets MR_D = MC_D and charges P from residual demand
- The fringe takes that price and supplies where P = MC
- Fringe entry shrinks residual demand and erodes the dominant firm's power
- 🧮 `D_D(P) = D(P) − S_F(P)`

**80. Cartels: OPEC vs CIPEC**
- Need a stable organisation (agreement, no cheating) and potential monopoly power
- Power depends on inelastic market demand and inelastic non-member supply
- OPEC: inelastic oil demand and non-OPEC supply — large price increases
- CIPEC: elastic copper demand, scrap and outside supply — little power
- Each member is tempted to cheat because P > its MC at the cartel output

**81. Dominant strategies and Nash equilibrium**
- Dominant strategy: best whatever the rival does
- Nash equilibrium: each player's choice is a best response to the others'
- Every dominant-strategy equilibrium is Nash, not vice versa
- Example matrix: advertising game where both advertise
- ✏️ Draw: 2×2 payoff matrix: (Adv, Adv) 10,5; (Adv, Don't) 15,0; (Don't, Adv) 6,8; (Don't, Don't) 10,2

**82. Prisoners' dilemma in trade policy**
- Protection is each country's dominant strategy
- (Protect, Protect) is the Nash equilibrium, worse for both than free trade
- Cooperation needs enforceable agreements (WTO), reciprocity, repeated interaction
- ✏️ Draw: 2×2 matrix, e.g. (Free, Free) 10,10; (Protect, Free) 20,−5; (Free, Protect) −5,20; (Protect, Protect) 0,0

**83. Repeated games and tit-for-tat**
- Tit-for-tat: cooperate first, then copy the rival's last move
- Infinite (or uncertain) repetition: the future punishment outweighs the one-off gain from cheating
- Needs a low discount rate, quick detection, credible punishment, few players
- With a known last round, cooperation unravels by backward induction

**84. Sequential games and backward induction**
- Extensive form (game tree) shows the order of moves
- Backward induction: solve the last move first and work back
- Subgame perfect equilibrium: Nash in every subgame; rules out empty threats
- Moving first can be an advantage (product-choice game)
- ✏️ Draw: Game tree with two stages and payoffs at the end nodes

**85. Credible vs empty threats; commitment**
- A threat is credible only if carrying it out is in the threatener's interest
- Irreversible investments change your own payoffs and make threats credible
- DuPont built titanium dioxide capacity ahead of demand to deter entry
- ✏️ Draw: Game tree: entry, then accommodate vs fight, with and without sunk capacity

**86. Entry deterrence with extra capacity**
- Stage 1: incumbent decides on capacity; stage 2: entrant decides; then pricing
- Extra capacity makes price war the best response after entry, so the threat is credible
- Deterrence pays if the capacity cost is below the profit it protects
- Otherwise accommodation is the profit-maximising strategy
- ✏️ Draw: Payoff matrix or tree with and without the capacity investment

**87. Brander–Spencer strategic trade policy (Boeing vs Airbus)**
- Either firm alone is profitable; if both produce, both lose
- Two Nash equilibria: whoever moves first wins
- A European subsidy makes producing Airbus's dominant strategy, so Boeing stays out
- Caveats: information demands, retaliation, subsidy wars
- ✏️ Draw: 2×2 matrix: both produce −10,−10; only one produces 100 for it, 0 for the other; neither 0,0. Then add a subsidy of 20 to Airbus

**88. Auctions and the winner's curse**
- English (ascending), Dutch (descending), first-price sealed, second-price sealed (Vickrey)
- Vickrey: bidding your true value is a dominant strategy
- Common value: item worth the same to all, but estimates differ (oil tracts)
- Winner's curse: the highest bidder usually overestimated, so shade your bid

**89. Derived demand; MRP vs VMP**
- Inputs are demanded for the output they help produce
- MRP_L = MP_L × MR; VMP_L = MP_L × P
- With output-market power MR < P, so MRP < VMP and the firm hires less
- 🧮 `MRP_L = MP_L·MR`
- 🧮 `VMP_L = MP_L·P`
- ✏️ Draw: MRP below VMP, both downward sloping

**90. Hiring rule MRP = w**
- An extra worker adds MRP to revenue and w to cost
- Hire while MRP > w; stop at MRP = w
- With a constant output price, labour demand slopes down because MP diminishes
- 🧮 `MRP_L = w  (competitive output: MP_L·P = w)`
- ✏️ Draw: MRP curve with horizontal wage lines and the chosen L

**91. Industry demand for an input**
- When the input price falls, all firms expand at once
- Output price falls, shifting each firm's MRP down
- Industry demand is less elastic than the sum of firms' MRPs
- ✏️ Draw: Firm MRP curves shifting left; the industry curve steeper than the simple sum

**92. Labour–leisure choice and backward-bending supply**
- The wage is the price of leisure: budget line between income and leisure (24 hours)
- Substitution effect of a higher wage: more work
- Income effect (leisure is normal): less work
- Supply bends backward where the income effect dominates (usually at high wages)
- ✏️ Draw: Upper panel: income–leisure budget lines; lower panel: the backward-bending supply curve

**93. Economic rent vs transfer earnings**
- Transfer earnings: the minimum needed to keep the input in its current use
- Economic rent: payment above that minimum
- Graphically: area above the supply curve and below the price
- With perfectly inelastic supply (prime land, unique talent) all payment is rent
- ✏️ Draw: Upward-sloping supply with rent above it; a vertical supply where the whole rectangle is rent

**94. Monopsony in the labour market**
- ME lies above AE (the labour supply curve)
- Hire where MRP = ME; pay the wage from the supply curve
- Fewer workers and lower wages than in a competitive market
- A minimum wage between wm and wc can raise employment
- ✏️ Draw: MRP, AE, ME with Lm, wm and the competitive Lc, wc (📊 monopsony)

**95. Union objectives**
- Union as a monopoly seller of labour facing the employers' labour demand
- Maximise employment: competitive wage and employment
- Maximise the wage bill: employ where MR of labour = 0 (unit-elastic point)
- Maximise members' rent: employ where MR of labour = supply — highest wage, fewest jobs
- ✏️ Draw: Labour demand, its MR curve and supply, with the three outcomes marked

**96. Bilateral monopoly**
- One buyer with monopsony power faces one seller with monopoly power
- The monopsonist wants a low price, the monopolist a high one
- The outcome is indeterminate between the two and depends on bargaining power
- The two powers tend to offset each other, often pushing the outcome toward the competitive one

**97. Stocks and flows**
- Stock: measured at a point in time (machines, factory capacity, inventory)
- Flow: measured per period (investment, depreciation, revenue, output)
- Investment adds to the capital stock; depreciation reduces it
- 🧮 `K(t+1) = K(t) + I(t) − depreciation(t)`

**98. Present discounted value**
- PDV: what a future payment is worth today
- A dollar today can earn interest, so future dollars are worth less even without inflation
- Higher R cuts PDV, especially for distant payments
- 🧮 `PDV = Σ FVₜ/(1 + R)ᵗ`

**99. Perpetuities and bond prices**
- Perpetuity: PV = C/R
- Bond price = PDV of coupons + PDV of face value
- Effective yield: the rate that makes PDV of payments equal the price
- Rates up → bond prices down, most for long maturities
- 🧮 `PV = C/R`
- 🧮 `P = Σ C/(1 + R)ᵗ + F/(1 + R)ᵀ`
- ✏️ Draw: Bond price falling as the interest rate rises

**100. NPV rule**
- Invest if NPV > 0
- NPV > 0 means the project earns more than the opportunity cost of capital
- Mutually exclusive projects: pick the higher NPV
- 🧮 `NPV = −C + Σ πₜ/(1 + R)ᵗ`
- ✏️ Draw: NPV against R: falling curve crossing zero at the IRR

**101. Fisher equation and consistent discounting**
- Nominal rate ≈ real rate + expected inflation
- Discount nominal cash flows at a nominal rate, real flows at a real rate
- Nominal flows at a real rate overstate NPV; real flows at a nominal rate understate it
- 🧮 `1 + i = (1 + r)(1 + π),  i ≈ r + π`

**102. Diversifiable vs systematic risk; beta**
- Diversifiable risk is firm-specific and disappears in a portfolio
- Only nondiversifiable (market) risk earns a premium
- Beta measures how much an asset moves with the market
- 🧮 `r_i = r_f + β(r_m − r_f)`

**103. Human capital and the NPV of a degree**
- Costs: tuition and forgone earnings while studying
- Benefits: wage premium over the working life
- Discount the stream; study if NPV > 0
- Ignore costs you would pay anyway and sunk past education
- 🧮 `NPV = −Σ (tuition + forgone wages)ₜ/(1 + R)ᵗ + Σ ΔWₜ/(1 + R)ᵗ`

**104. Hotelling's rule and user cost**
- A resource in the ground is an asset: its net price must rise at the rate of interest
- Otherwise producers shift extraction earlier or later
- User cost: opportunity cost of extracting now rather than later; equals P − MC under competition
- In practice user cost is small; discoveries, technology and market power dominate prices
- 🧮 `(P − MC) grows at rate R`
- 🧮 `P = MC + user cost`

**105. Partial vs general equilibrium**
- Partial: one market, others held fixed
- General: prices and quantities of related markets determined together, with feedback
- Example: an ethanol tariff raises demand for domestic ethanol and corn, then feed and food prices, then feeds back
- ✏️ Draw: Two markets side by side with shifts and the feedback arrows

**106. Edgeworth box and the contract curve**
- Box dimensions = total endowments of the two goods; each point is an allocation
- If MRS_A ≠ MRS_B, indifference curves cross: trade can help both
- Trade continues until tangency; the contract curve links all tangencies
- 🧮 `Efficiency: MRS_A = MRS_B`
- ✏️ Draw: Edgeworth box with endowment point, the lens of mutually better allocations and the contract curve

**107. First welfare theorem**
- A competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient
- All consumers face the same prices and set MRS = Px/Py, so MRSs are equal
- Prices coordinate without central knowledge (invisible hand)
- Holds only without market power, externalities, public goods or information problems
- 🧮 `MRS_A = Px/Py = MRS_B`

**108. Utility possibilities frontier; equity vs efficiency**
- UPF: utility levels of the two consumers along the contract curve
- Points inside are inefficient; beyond are unattainable
- An efficient point can be less fair than an inefficient interior point
- Redistribution through distorting taxes trades some efficiency for equity
- ✏️ Draw: UPF with an inefficient interior point and two frontier points, one very unequal

**109. Production efficiency and the PPF**
- Production Edgeworth box: labour and capital split between industries
- Technical efficiency: equal MRTS in both industries
- The production contract curve maps into the PPF
- The PPF is concave because inputs are not equally suited to both goods
- 🧮 `MRTS_X = MRTS_Y`
- ✏️ Draw: Production box with the contract curve; PPF derived from it

**110. MRT and output efficiency**
- MRT: Y given up for one more X — the slope of the PPF
- MRT = MCx/MCy
- Output efficiency: MRS = MRT; if MRS > MRT produce more X
- Competition: MRS = Px/Py = MCx/MCy = MRT
- 🧮 `MRT = MCx/MCy`
- 🧮 `MRS = MRT`
- ✏️ Draw: PPF tangent to an indifference curve at the efficient output mix

**111. Gains from trade beyond the PPF**
- Specialise where domestic MRT equals the world price ratio
- Trade along the world price line to a consumption point outside the PPF
- Comparative (not absolute) advantage drives gains: opportunity costs differ
- A country less productive in everything still gains
- ✏️ Draw: PPF, the world price line tangent at the production point, consumption point outside the PPF

**112. Four sources of market failure**
- Market power: P > MC, output too low
- Incomplete or asymmetric information: lemons, adverse selection
- Externalities: costs or benefits outside prices
- Public goods: non-rival and non-excludable, so markets underprovide

**113. Negative externalities**
- External cost borne by third parties
- MSC = MC + MEC lies above private MC
- Market produces where D = MC (Q₁), more than the efficient Q* where D = MSC
- Deadweight loss between Q* and Q₁
- 🧮 `MSC = MC + MEC`
- ✏️ Draw: D, MC, MSC, MEC with Q*, Q₁ and the DWL triangle (📊 externality)

**114. Emissions fees vs standards**
- Fee = MEC at the efficient output makes the firm produce Q*
- With full information, a fee and a standard reach the same level
- Fees give continuous incentives to cut more and innovate
- With uncertainty: steep damage curve favours standards, steep abatement cost favours fees
- 🧮 `Fee t = MEC(Q*)`
- ✏️ Draw: Externality diagram with the fee shifting MC up to meet D at Q* (📊 externality)

**115. Tradable emissions permits (cap-and-trade)**
- The state caps total emissions; firms trade permits
- Low-cost abaters cut more and sell; high-cost abaters buy
- Marginal abatement costs equalise at the permit price, minimising total cost
- Initial allocation affects who pays whom, not efficiency (EU ETS)
- 🧮 `MCA_1 = MCA_2 = permit price`
- ✏️ Draw: Two firms' MCA curves with the permit price and the abatement split

**116. Coase theorem**
- Clear property rights and no transaction costs: bargaining gives the efficient outcome
- Who holds the right affects who pays whom, not the outcome
- Obstacles: many parties, free riders, holdouts, hidden information, high bargaining costs
- Works best with few parties (factory and fishery)

**117. Positive externalities**
- External benefit to third parties
- MSB = D + MEB lies above private demand
- The market underproduces (R&D, vaccines, green technology)
- Fix: a subsidy equal to MEB at the efficient output
- 🧮 `MSB = D + MEB`
- ✏️ Draw: MC, D, MSB with market Q₁ below efficient Q*

**118. Common property and the tragedy of the commons**
- Rival but non-excludable (fisheries, crowded shipping lanes)
- Each user ignores the cost imposed on others, so the resource is overused
- Open-access level exceeds the social optimum where MSC = MB
- Fixes: ownership, licences, tradable quotas
- ✏️ Draw: Demand (MB), private cost, social cost; open-access F_c beyond the efficient F*

**119. Public goods**
- Non-rival: one person's use does not reduce others'
- Non-excludable: non-payers cannot be kept out
- Examples: defence, lighthouses and navigation aids, basic research
- Markets underprovide because of free riding

**120. Free riders and vertical summation**
- Free rider: benefits without paying, so voluntary provision falls short
- Public good: everyone consumes the same quantity, so add marginal benefits vertically
- Private good: add quantities horizontally
- Efficient level: sum of marginal benefits = marginal cost
- 🧮 `Σ MBᵢ = MC`
- ✏️ Draw: Two individual demand curves, their vertical sum and the MC line

## Charts

- **ceiling** — Price ceiling in a competitive market. Curves: D, S. Areas: A, B, C. Ceiling Pmax below P₀: firms supply Q₁, buyers want Q₂ — shortage Q₂ − Q₁.
- **tax** — A specific (per-unit) tax. Curves: D, S. Areas: A, D, B, C. Tax t = Pb − Ps. Buyers pay Pb, sellers keep Ps, quantity falls from Q₀ to Q₁.
- **tariff** — Import tariff in a small importing country. Curves: D, S (domestic), Pw, Pw + t. Areas: A, B, D, C. Free trade at Pw: imports Qd − Qs. Tariff t raises the domestic price to P*: imports shrink to Q′d − Q′s.
- **firmCosts** — Short-run costs of a competitive firm. Curves: MC, ATC, AVC, firm supply. MC cuts AVC and ATC at their minimum points.
- **monopoly** — Monopoly vs perfect competition. Curves: D = AR, MR, MC. Areas: A, B, C. The monopolist picks Qm where MR = MC and charges Pm read off the demand curve.
- **monopsony** — Monopsony (a single buyer of an input). Curves: S = AE, ME, MV (demand). Areas: A, B, C. The monopsonist hires Lm where ME = MV and pays the wage wm read off the supply (AE) curve.
- **kinked** — Sweezy kinked demand curve. Curves: D, MR, MR, MC, MC′. Rivals match price cuts but not price rises, so demand is flatter above P* than below it.
- **cournot** — Cournot duopoly: reaction curves. Curves: Firm 1 reaction curve, Firm 2 reaction curve, collusion curve, competitive outcomes. Market demand P = 30 − Q, both firms have MC = 0 (Pindyck's example).
- **externality** — Negative production externality. Curves: D, MC (private supply), MSC = MC + MEC, MEC. Areas: DWL. The market produces Q₁ where D = MC; the efficient output Q* is where D = MSC.
- **consumerChoice** — Consumer optimum: budget line and indifference curves. Curves: budget line, U₁, U₂, U₃. Px = 2, Py = 1, income 20, so the budget line has slope −Px/Py = −2. Utility U = X·Y, so MRS = Y/X.
