@exam 1 | Market mechanism, supply shocks and shortages
- Excess demand bids the price up, excess supply pushes it down, until Qd = Qs
- A supply disruption (strike, war) shifts supply left: price rises, quantity falls
- That excess demand is temporary, because the price is free to rise and clear the market
- A binding price ceiling blocks the price rise, so the shortage persists and rationing appears
> Two panels: supply shifting left with a new higher equilibrium; a ceiling below P₀ with the shortage Q₂ − Q₁ [chart:ceiling]

@exam 2 | Movement along vs shift of the supply curve
- Movement along: caused only by a change in the good's own price
- Shift: caused by costs, technology, capacity, input prices, number of sellers, policy
- Oil example — movement: higher world price makes shale firms drill more wells
- Oil example — shift: cheaper fracking technology (right) or OPEC+ quota cuts (left)
> One supply curve with an arrow along it; a second panel with S shifting to S′

@exam 3 | Price elasticity, point vs arc, slope, maximum spending
- Ep = % change in Q ÷ % change in P; it has no units
- Point elasticity uses one point; arc elasticity uses midpoints so the direction does not matter
- On a straight line the slope is constant but P/Q changes, so elasticity changes along the line
- Spending P·Q peaks at the midpoint, where |E| = 1: elastic above, inelastic below
= Ep = (ΔQ/ΔP)·(P/Q)
= Arc: E = (ΔQ/ΔP)·(P̄/Q̄)
= Linear demand Q = a − bP: E = −bP/Q
> Straight demand line marked "elastic" above the midpoint, "|E| = 1" at the midpoint, "inelastic" below

@exam 4 | Income and cross-price elasticity (EV example)
- Income elasticity: < 0 inferior, 0–1 necessity, > 1 luxury
- Cross-price elasticity: > 0 substitutes, < 0 complements, 0 unrelated
- +0.8 between imported EVs and gasoline cars: substitutes — rivals' price moves shift your sales
- +2.2 for luxury EVs: sales grow faster than income and swing with the business cycle; target rich, fast-growing markets
= E_I = (ΔQ/Q)/(ΔI/I)
= E_XY = (ΔQx/Qx)/(ΔPy/Py)

@exam 5 | Short-run vs long-run demand elasticity: gasoline vs aircraft
- Nondurables (gasoline): demand is more elastic in the long run — people change cars, homes, habits
- Durables (cars, aircraft): demand is more elastic in the short run — buyers can postpone replacement
- In the long run the stock of durables must be maintained, so purchases recover
- Gasoline has greater long-run elasticity; aircraft orders swing sharply in the short run
> Two demand curves for each good: steep SR and flat LR for gasoline; the reverse for aircraft

@exam 6 | Long-run supply elasticity and secondary supply (scrap)
- Short run: capacity (mines, smelters, land) is fixed, so supply is inelastic
- Long run: new capacity comes on stream, so supply is much more elastic
- Secondary supply (scrap) is more elastic in the short run than in the long run: high prices pull scrap out, but the stock runs down
- Recycling raises the short-run elasticity of total supply (Pindyck's copper table)
> Steep short-run supply and flatter long-run supply through the same point

@exam 7 | Copper demand shift (calculation task) | problem
- Method: set QD = QS for the original equilibrium, then scale demand by 1.2 and solve again
- "20% more at every price" multiplies the whole demand function: both intercept and slope
- Original: 32 − 0.20P = 18 + 0.12P gives P = 43.75, Q = 23.25
- New: 38.4 − 0.24P = 18 + 0.12P gives P ≈ 56.67, Q = 24.8 (price +30%, quantity +7%)
- Price rises much more than quantity because supply is relatively inelastic
= Q′D = 1.2·(32 − 0.20P) = 38.4 − 0.24P
> Supply line with demand shifting right; label old and new equilibrium

@exam 8 | Price ceiling on an essential import
- Below equilibrium, importers bring in less while buyers want more: a shortage
- Gainers: buyers who still get the good at the low price
- Losers: rationed-out buyers, importers and foreign suppliers; deadweight loss B + C
- Price no longer rations, so queues, coupons, favouritism or black markets do
> Ceiling below P₀; mark Q₁ (supplied), Q₂ (demanded), areas A, B, C [chart:ceiling]

@exam 9 | Preference assumptions; indifference curves cannot cross
- Completeness, transitivity, more is better, diminishing MRS (convexity)
- Utility is ordinal: it only ranks bundles
- If two curves crossed, transitivity would make two bundles equally good even though one has more of both goods — contradicting "more is better"
> Two crossing indifference curves with points A, B, D to show the contradiction

@exam 10 | Marginal rate of substitution
- MRS of X for Y: the most Y a consumer gives up for one more X and stays equally well off
- It is the absolute slope of the indifference curve
- Diminishing MRS: the more X you have, the less Y you give up for more X — convex curves
= MRS = −ΔY/ΔX = MUx/MUy
> Convex indifference curve with two slope triangles getting flatter to the right [chart:consumerChoice]

@exam 11 | Special preferences: substitutes, complements, bads, neutral goods
- Perfect substitutes: straight lines, constant MRS; optimum usually a corner
- Perfect complements (Leontief): L-shapes; optimum at the kink; no substitution effect
- A bad (pollution): upward-sloping curves; consumer chooses zero of it if possible
- Neutral good X: horizontal lines (MRS = 0); all income goes to Y
= Substitutes U = aX + bY: MRS = a/b
= Complements U = min(aX, bY): MRS undefined at the kink
> Four small panels with the four shapes and the optimum on each

@exam 12 | Budget constraint and how it shifts
- Budget line: I = Px·X + Py·Y; intercepts I/Px and I/Py
- Slope = −Px/Py, the market rate of exchange
- 20% inflation in all prices, nominal income fixed: parallel inward shift (slope unchanged)
- 10% tariff on X only: rotates inward around the Y-intercept, slope −1.1·Px/Py
= Y = I/Py − (Px/Py)·X
> Original line; a parallel inward line; a line pivoted around the Y-intercept

@exam 13 | Utility maximisation: MRS = Px/Py
- At an interior optimum the budget line is tangent to the highest reachable indifference curve
- Equal marginal principle: the last dollar on each good gives the same utility
- MRS > Px/Py: the consumer values X above its price, so buys more X and less Y until MRS falls
= MRS = MUx/MUy = Px/Py
= MUx/Px = MUy/Py
> Budget line tangent to U₂ at A; points B, C on U₁ with arrows toward A [chart:consumerChoice]

@exam 14 | Corner solutions
- A corner solution: the consumer buys none of one good
- It arises when MRS ≠ Px/Py even at the edge of the budget line (e.g. perfect substitutes)
- Airlines as perfect substitutes (MRS = 1): all flights go to the cheaper airline; any mix only if prices are equal
= Corner at X = 0 when MRS < Px/Py for every bundle
> Straight-line indifference curves meeting the budget line at an axis

@exam 15 | Revealed preference and WARP
- If A is chosen when B is affordable, A is revealed preferred to B
- Revealed preference builds preferences from observed choices
- WARP: if A is revealed preferred to B, B must never be chosen when A is also affordable
- Choosing B later is consistent only if A has become unaffordable
> Two budget lines: A chosen on the first, B on the second; shade the revealed-preferred region

@exam 16 | Laspeyres vs Paasche and CPI bias
- Laspeyres uses base-period quantities: it overstates the cost-of-living increase
- Paasche uses current-period quantities: it understates it
- Bias comes from substitution toward goods that became relatively cheaper
- Other CPI biases: quality changes and new goods added late; chain-weighting reduces the bias
= LI = Σ P₁Q₀ / Σ P₀Q₀
= PI = Σ P₁Q₁ / Σ P₀Q₁

@exam 17 | Price-consumption curve and individual demand
- Vary Px with income and Py fixed; link the tangencies to form the PCC
- Plot each Px against the X chosen: the individual demand curve
- With Y as "all other goods": downward PCC = elastic, horizontal = unit elastic, upward = inelastic demand
> Upper panel: budget lines pivoting with three tangencies; lower panel: the derived demand curve

@exam 18 | Income-consumption curve and Engel curves
- Shift the budget line out in parallel; link the tangencies to form the ICC
- Engel curve: quantity of X at each income level
- Necessity 0 < E_I < 1 (budget share falls), luxury E_I > 1 (share rises), inferior E_I < 0 (Engel curve bends back)
= E_I = (ΔQ/Q)/(ΔI/I)
> ICC panel above, three Engel curves below: necessity, luxury, inferior

@exam 19 | Hicksian decomposition: substitution and income effects
- Draw a hypothetical budget line with the new slope, tangent to the original indifference curve
- Substitution effect: move along U₁ to that tangency — always opposite to the price change
- Income effect: move from the hypothetical line to the new optimum; same direction for a normal good
- Convex indifference curves guarantee the negative substitution effect
> Original and pivoted budget lines, the dashed hypothetical line, points A → B (SE) → C (IE)

@exam 20 | Inferior vs Giffen goods
- Inferior: demand falls as income rises (negative income effect)
- Giffen: inferior AND the income effect outweighs the substitution effect, so demand slopes up
- Every Giffen good is inferior; most inferior goods are not Giffen
- Plausible only for staples taking a large budget share of very poor households (Jensen & Miller, rice in China)
> Decomposition where the income effect arrow is longer and points opposite to the substitution effect

@exam 21 | Horizontal summation and market demand
- Market demand: add quantities demanded by all buyers at each price
- New foreign buyers shift market demand to the right
- Kinks appear where a new buyer's choke price is reached and their demand is added
> Two individual demands and their horizontal sum with a kink

@exam 22 | Consumer surplus; tariffs and transport costs
- Consumer surplus: willingness to pay minus the price paid, summed over units
- Graphically: area under demand and above price
- A tariff or higher transport cost raises price: CS falls by a trapezoid (rectangle on units still bought + triangle on units given up)
= CS ≈ ½·(Pmax − P)·Q for linear demand
> Demand with CS triangle; a higher price line showing the lost trapezoid

@exam 23 | Network externalities: bandwagon and snob effects
- Bandwagon (positive): more users make the good more valuable; a price cut draws users, which shifts demand out again — more elastic
- Snob (negative): more owners reduce exclusivity; a price cut's effect is partly offset — less elastic
- Examples: messaging and payment platforms (bandwagon); luxury watches and limited editions (snob)
> Demand curves for 20k, 40k, 60k users; market demand flatter (bandwagon) or steeper (snob)

@exam 24 | Speculative demand
- Demand driven by expected future price rises, not by the good's direct use
- Rising prices can raise expected prices, shifting demand right: a feedback loop
- The law of demand still holds for utility-based demand; expectations are a shift factor
- Examples: housing booms, commodity hoarding — bubbles end when expectations turn
> Demand shifting right repeatedly as price rises

@exam 25 | Production function; short run vs long run
- Production function: maximum output from each input combination with given technology
- Short run: at least one input fixed (usually capital); long run: all inputs variable
- An asset is fixed when it cannot be changed within the planning horizon (fab construction takes years)
- As the horizon lengthens, fixed inputs become variable
= q = F(K, L)

@exam 26 | TP, AP and MP; why MP cuts AP at its maximum
- AP = q/L; MP = Δq/ΔL
- MP above AP pulls AP up; MP below AP pulls it down, so MP = AP at AP's maximum
- Graph: AP = slope of a ray from the origin to TP; MP = slope of TP
- TP is at its maximum where MP = 0
= dAP/dL = (MP − AP)/L
> TP curve above; AP and MP below, with MP crossing AP at AP's peak

@exam 27 | Diminishing marginal returns
- With other inputs fixed, the marginal product of an input eventually falls
- Output still rises as long as MP > 0 — it just rises more slowly
- Better technology shifts TP upward and delays diminishing returns (Malthus was wrong)
- Applies to one input at a time, not to scaling all inputs
> TP curve shifting up from O₁ to O₂ to O₃ with technological progress

@exam 28 | Isoquants and isoquant maps
- Isoquant: all input combinations giving the same output
- Downward sloping (both MPs positive), convex (diminishing MRTS), never crossing
- An upward-sloping section means one input has a negative MP — an uneconomic region
> Isoquant map with q₁ < q₂ < q₃; a bent-back section outside the ridge lines

@exam 29 | MRTS = MPL/MPK
- MRTS: how much capital can be replaced by one more unit of labour at constant output
- Output constant along the isoquant gives the ratio of marginal products
- MRTS diminishes: more labour lowers MPL and raises MPK
= MPL·ΔL + MPK·ΔK = 0
= MRTS = −ΔK/ΔL = MPL/MPK
> Convex isoquant with flatter slope triangles moving right

@exam 30 | Linear, Leontief and Cobb-Douglas technologies
- Linear (perfect substitutes): straight isoquants, elasticity of substitution σ = ∞
- Fixed proportions (Leontief): L-shaped isoquants, σ = 0
- Cobb-Douglas: smooth convex isoquants, σ = 1
- High σ makes a firm resilient to shocks in one input; Leontief firms cannot switch
= q = aL + bK;  q = min(aL, bK);  q = A·K^α·L^β
= σ = %Δ(K/L) / %ΔMRTS
> Three isoquant panels side by side

@exam 31 | Returns to scale vs diminishing returns
- Increasing, constant, decreasing RTS: output rises by more, the same or less than the proportional rise in all inputs
- Diminishing returns concern one input; returns to scale concern all inputs together
- Example q = K^0.6·L^0.6: each MP diminishes (0.6 < 1), but 0.6 + 0.6 = 1.2 > 1 gives IRS
= F(tK, tL) = t^(α+β)·F(K, L)
> Isoquants for q = 10, 20, 30 getting closer together along a ray (IRS)

@exam 32 | Economies and diseconomies of scale in global industries
- Sources: huge indivisible fixed costs (fabs, design, certification), specialisation, volume purchasing
- Chips, cars, aerospace: few producers worldwide, because minimum efficient scale is huge
- Diseconomies: layers of hierarchy, slow decisions, weak incentives, information loss
- Scale economies concern all inputs; external input-price effects are a separate idea
> U-shaped LAC with economies, minimum efficient scale and diseconomies marked

@exam 33 | Accounting, economic and opportunity cost
- Accounting cost: explicit outlays and depreciation
- Economic cost = opportunity cost: includes implicit costs
- A firm's own building: no rent in the books, but forgone rental income is an economic cost
- Owner's equity: forgone return elsewhere is a cost, so accounting profit can hide an economic loss
= Economic profit = revenue − explicit costs − implicit costs

@exam 34 | Sunk costs
- Sunk cost: already spent and unrecoverable
- Ignore sunk costs: only future costs and benefits matter for the decision
- Example: spent $50m, need $20m more to earn $30m — finish it
- Not sunk: assets with resale value, avoidable future costs

@exam 35 | Short-run cost curves
- TC = FC + VC; ATC = AFC + AVC
- MC = ΔVC/Δq, so fixed cost does not affect MC
- MC cuts AVC and ATC at their minimum points; AFC falls continuously
= MC = ΔTC/Δq = ΔVC/Δq = w/MPL
> U-shaped MC, ATC and AVC with MC crossing both at their minimums [chart:firmCosts]

@exam 36 | Isocost lines and cost minimisation
- Isocost: C = wL + rK, slope −w/r
- Lowest cost for a given output: isocost tangent to the isoquant
- If MPL/w > MPK/r, use more labour and less capital
= MRTS = MPL/MPK = w/r
= MPL/w = MPK/r
> Isoquant with three isocost lines; tangency at the cheapest one

@exam 37 | Expansion path after a wage increase
- Expansion path: cost-minimising input mixes for each output level
- If w doubles with r fixed, isocosts get steeper: the firm substitutes capital for labour
- K/L rises; the expansion path rotates toward the capital axis
- How far it rotates depends on the elasticity of substitution
> Isoquants with old and new tangencies; old and new expansion paths from the origin

@exam 38 | LAC as the envelope of SAC curves
- For each output, the long-run choice is the plant with the lowest SAC
- LAC touches each SAC once and never lies above any SAC
- With economies of scale the tangency is left of the SAC minimum; with diseconomies to its right
- Only at the bottom of LAC does tangency fall on an SAC's minimum
> Several SAC curves with LAC as their lower envelope

@exam 39 | Economies of scope
- Joint production of two products costs less than producing them separately
- Different from scale (volume of one product) and from learning
- Sources: shared networks, hubs, IT, brands, R&D (logistics, consumer electronics)
- SC > 0 means economies of scope; SC < 0 diseconomies
= SC = [C(q₁) + C(q₂) − C(q₁, q₂)] / C(q₁, q₂)

@exam 40 | Learning curve
- Cost per unit falls as cumulative output rises (workers, managers and engineers learn)
- Learning shifts cost curves down over time; scale moves along LAC with the output rate
- 80% curve: each doubling of cumulative output cuts labour hours per unit to 80%
- Rationale for aggressive early pricing (aircraft, chips)
= L = A + B·N^(−β)
> Labour hours per unit falling with cumulative output; LAC₁ shifting down to LAC₂

@exam 41 | Perfect competition: conditions and horizontal firm demand
- Price taking, homogeneous product, free entry and exit
- The firm's output is tiny, and buyers can switch to identical goods
- So firm demand is perfectly elastic at the market price, while market demand slopes down
> Two panels: market S and D setting P; the firm's horizontal d = MR = P

@exam 42 | MR = MC and P = MC
- Profit π(q) = R(q) − C(q) peaks where dπ/dq = MR − MC = 0
- If MR > MC, expand; if MR < MC, contract
- For a price taker MR = P, so P = MC; MC must be rising (second-order condition)
= dπ/dq = MR − MC = 0
> Firm diagram: P line crossing rising MC at q*

@exam 43 | Short-run losses and the shutdown rule
- Produce at a loss if P ≥ AVCmin: revenue covers variable cost and part of fixed cost
- Shut down if P < AVCmin
- Fixed costs are paid either way, so they do not affect the decision
- Long-run exit if P < ATC persists
= Shut down if P < AVCmin  (equivalently R < VC)
> Cost curves with prices above ATCmin, between AVCmin and ATCmin, and below AVCmin [chart:firmCosts]

@exam 44 | The firm's short-run supply curve
- For each price, the firm produces where P = MC
- Below AVCmin it produces nothing
- So supply = MC curve above AVCmin
- A higher variable input price shifts MC up and supply left; fixed costs do not shift it
> The bold part of MC above the shutdown point [chart:firmCosts]

@exam 45 | Short-run market supply
- With constant input prices: horizontal sum of firms' supply curves
- If input prices rise as the whole industry expands, each MC shifts up
- So industry supply is steeper than the simple horizontal sum
> Three firms' MC curves and their horizontal sum

@exam 46 | Producer surplus vs profit
- Producer surplus: sum over units of (P − MC); area above supply, below price
- PS = profit + FC = TR − VC
- In the long run (no fixed costs) PS and profit coincide
- Market PS: area above market supply and below price
= PS = R − VC;  π = R − VC − FC
> Firm panel and market panel with PS shaded

@exam 47 | Long-run competitive equilibrium
- Profits attract entry, losses cause exit, shifting market supply
- Long run: zero economic profit, P = MC = LACmin
- Zero economic profit = normal return; accounting profit is positive
- Owners stay because they earn their opportunity cost
= P = MC = LACmin
> Market supply shifting right to S′ and the firm at the bottom of LAC

@exam 48 | Long-run industry supply: constant, increasing, decreasing cost
- Constant-cost: input prices unchanged as the industry grows — horizontal supply at LACmin
- Increasing-cost: scarce inputs get dearer — upward-sloping supply
- Decreasing-cost: suppliers' scale economies cut input prices — downward-sloping supply
- External (pecuniary) economies and diseconomies decide which case applies
> Demand shifting right with three different long-run supply curves

@exam 49 | Efficiency and the deadweight loss of a price ceiling
- Efficiency: competitive equilibrium maximises CS + PS
- Under a ceiling: ΔCS = A − B, ΔPS = −A − C, deadweight loss = B + C
- DWL is largest with elastic supply and inelastic demand; zero with perfectly inelastic supply
- With very inelastic demand, consumers as a group can lose (B > A)
= DWL = B + C
> Ceiling diagram with areas A, B, C [chart:ceiling]

@exam 50 | Price floors and minimum wages
- A floor above equilibrium creates excess supply (unemployment for a minimum wage)
- Workers who keep jobs gain; employers and priced-out workers lose
- Deadweight loss on trades that no longer happen
- If producers make more than they can sell, the cost of unsold output can wipe out their gain
> Floor above P₀ with Qd, Qs and the excess supply; triangles B, C

@exam 51 | Price supports vs direct subsidies
- Support: the state buys the surplus (Q₂ − Q₁) at Ps
- Consumers lose, producers gain, taxpayers pay for unwanted output
- Total cost to consumers and taxpayers far exceeds producers' gain
- A direct cash transfer of the same gain is much cheaper and creates no waste
= Cost to state = Ps·(Q₂ − Q₁)
> Support price above P₀; consumer loss A + B, producer gain A + B + D, state purchases

@exam 52 | Production quotas and licences (taxi medallions)
- A cap on quantity raises price above MC
- Consumers lose; incumbents with licences gain the rents
- Lost trades are a deadweight loss
- Tradable licences capture the rent in their price (New York medallions)
> Vertical supply cap at Q₁ below Q₀; areas A (transfer), B + C (DWL)

@exam 53 | Import tariff in a small importing country
- Tariff raises the domestic price from Pw to Pw + t
- Consumers lose A + B + C + D; producers gain A; state revenue D
- Deadweight loss B (production distortion) + C (consumption distortion)
= Revenue D = t·(Q′d − Q′s)
> World price line, tariff line, areas A, B, D, C [chart:tariff]

@exam 54 | Quota vs tariff; quota rents
- A quota with the same import volume raises the price by the same amount
- If the state auctions licences (or domestic firms hold them), the loss is still B + C
- If foreign exporters get the quota rent (VERs), the country also loses D
- Example: Japan's car export restraints to the US in the 1980s
> Same diagram as the tariff, with D labelled "quota rent" [chart:tariff]

@exam 55 | Tax incidence and pass-through
- A tax opens a wedge Pb − Ps = t
- The less elastic side bears more of the burden
- Statutory incidence does not matter: taxing buyers or sellers gives the same result
- Revenue A + D, deadweight loss B + C
= Buyers' share = Es/(Es − Ed)
= Sellers' share = −Ed/(Es − Ed)
> Tax wedge diagram with Pb, Ps, Q₁ and areas A, D, B, C [chart:tax]

@exam 56 | Per-unit subsidy
- A subsidy s creates a wedge Ps − Pb = s
- Output expands past the efficient level: extra units cost more than buyers value them
- Taxpayers' cost exceeds the gain in CS + PS: a deadweight loss
- Benefit split by the same elasticity rule as a tax
= Buyers' share = Es/(Es − Ed)
> Supply and demand with Ps above and Pb below P₀, quantity Q₁ > Q₀, DWL triangle

@exam 57 | MR, price and elasticity; why monopoly avoids inelastic demand
- Selling one more unit brings in P but lowers the price on all units
- MR is positive only where demand is elastic (|Ed| > 1)
- On the inelastic part MR < 0: cutting output raises revenue and lowers cost
= MR = P + Q·(ΔP/ΔQ) = P·(1 + 1/Ed)
> Linear demand with MR twice as steep, MR = 0 at the midpoint

@exam 58 | Lerner index and the markup rule
- Lerner index measures monopoly power: 0 under competition
- Profit maximisation gives the inverse-elasticity rule
- Import substitutes make the firm's demand more elastic and shrink its markup
= L = (P − MC)/P = −1/Ed
= P = MC / (1 + 1/Ed)

@exam 59 | Sources of monopoly power and barriers to entry
- Elasticity of market demand
- Number of firms in the market
- Interaction among firms
- Barriers: patents, copyrights, licences, scale economies, control of key inputs

@exam 60 | Deadweight loss of monopoly and rent-seeking
- Monopoly output Qm < Qc, price Pm > Pc
- Consumers lose A + B, producer gains A − C, deadweight loss B + C
- Rent-seeking (lobbying to win or keep monopoly) can waste much of A as well
= Deadweight loss = B + C
> Demand, MR, MC with Pm, Qm, Pc, Qc and areas A, B, C [chart:monopoly]

@exam 61 | Price ceiling on a monopolist
- Below the cap, MR equals the cap price up to the demand curve
- A cap at the competitive price raises output to Qc and removes the deadweight loss
- A cap set too low cuts output and creates a shortage
- In a competitive industry a ceiling always reduces output
> Monopoly diagram with a horizontal cap line and the new kinked MR

@exam 62 | Natural monopoly and regulation
- Subadditive costs: one firm can serve the market more cheaply than several
- Typically LAC keeps falling over the relevant range (grids, water, rail)
- P = MC is efficient but below AC: the firm loses money
- P = AC lets it break even but leaves a small deadweight loss
> Falling AC with MC below it; mark Pm, P = AC and P = MC outcomes

@exam 63 | Monopsony: average vs marginal expenditure
- Monopsony: a single (or dominant) buyer
- AE is the supply curve the buyer faces
- To buy one more unit it must pay more on all units, so ME > AE
= ME = AE + Q·(ΔAE/ΔQ)
> Upward-sloping AE and a steeper ME above it [chart:monopsony]

@exam 64 | Monopsony equilibrium and deadweight loss
- Buy where MV = ME, then pay the AE (supply) price
- Less is bought at a lower price than under competition
- Buyer gains A − B, sellers lose A + C, deadweight loss B + C
- Regional labour markets with one big employer
= MV = ME
> MV, AE, ME with Qm, Pm and areas A, B, C [chart:monopsony]

@exam 65 | Why a single price leaves profit on the table
- Some buyers would pay more than the single price (uncaptured consumer surplus)
- Some would buy at prices above MC but below the single price (deadweight loss)
- Price discrimination aims to capture both
> Monopoly diagram highlighting CS above Pm and the DWL triangle

@exam 66 | First-degree price discrimination
- Charge each buyer their reservation price
- Needs knowledge of willingness to pay and no resale
- Demand becomes the MR curve, so output rises to where D = MC
- No deadweight loss, but all consumer surplus goes to the firm
> Demand and MC with the whole area between them shaded as profit

@exam 67 | Second-degree price discrimination
- Different unit prices for different quantities (block pricing, quantity discounts)
- Self-selection: buyers choose their block or plan, revealing their type
- Utilities and telecoms extract surplus without knowing individual demand
> Stepped price schedule P₁, P₂, P₃ over blocks of quantity

@exam 68 | Third-degree price discrimination
- Separate groups (countries, students) with different elasticities
- Allocate output so that MR is equal across markets and equal to MC
- Higher price where demand is less elastic
= MR₁ = MR₂ = MC
= P₁/P₂ = (1 + 1/E₂)/(1 + 1/E₁)
> Two market panels plus combined MR_T crossing MC

@exam 69 | Intertemporal discrimination vs peak-load pricing
- Intertemporal: high launch price for eager buyers, lower later (electronics, hardcover then paperback)
- Peak-load: higher prices at peak times because MC is higher when capacity is stretched
- Peak-load pricing is efficient (P ≈ MC in each period), not surplus extraction
= Peak-load: MR = MC in each period
> Two demand curves (peak, off-peak) with the same rising MC

@exam 70 | Two-part tariff
- Price = entry fee T + usage price P per unit
- Identical consumers: P = MC, T = each consumer's consumer surplus
- Different consumers: P > MC and a lower T, trading off entry fees against usage margins
- Examples: amusement parks, gyms, razors and blades, phone plans
= Profit = n·T + (P − MC)·Q

@exam 71 | Pure and mixed bundling
- Pure: only the package is sold; mixed: package and items separately
- Bundling pays when reservation prices are negatively correlated
- Mixed bundling beats pure when MC is significant and some buyers value one item below cost
- Examples: software suites, media and streaming licences
> Reservation-price scatter (r₁, r₂) with a negative correlation and the bundle price line

@exam 72 | Tying as metering
- Buyers of the main product must buy the firm's supplies
- Usage intensity reveals willingness to pay, so heavy users pay more
- Examples: printers and cartridges, Xerox paper, IBM punch cards
- Also used to protect quality (franchising) — watched by competition authorities

@exam 73 | Monopolistic competition and long-run tangency
- Many firms, differentiated products, free entry
- Short-run profits attract entry; each firm's demand shifts in
- Long run: demand tangent to LAC — zero profit, P > MC
= Long run: MR = MC and P = LAC
> Short-run panel with profit; long-run panel with tangency

@exam 74 | Excess capacity theorem
- Tangency is on the falling part of LAC, so output is below minimum efficient scale
- Costs are higher than the minimum, and P > MC
- Trade-off: the inefficiency is the price of variety consumers value
- Small if products are close substitutes (very elastic demand)
> LAC with the tangency point left of its minimum

@exam 75 | Cournot duopoly
- Firms choose quantities simultaneously, each taking the other's output as given
- Reaction function: best output for each rival output
- Nash equilibrium where reaction curves cross
- Output between monopoly and competition; price between too
= Example P = 30 − Q, MC = 0: Q₁ = 15 − Q₂/2;  Q₁ = Q₂ = 10, P = 10
> Reaction curves crossing at 10, 10 with collusion and competition lines [chart:cournot]

@exam 76 | Bertrand price competition
- Firms choose prices simultaneously; identical products
- Any price above MC can be undercut to win the whole market
- So P = MC even with two firms (Bertrand paradox)
- Resolved by capacity constraints, differentiation or repeated interaction
= Equilibrium: P₁ = P₂ = MC

@exam 77 | Stackelberg leadership
- The leader commits to output first; the follower responds on its reaction curve
- The leader picks the point on the follower's reaction curve that maximises its own profit
- First-mover advantage: leader produces more and earns more than in Cournot; follower less
= Example: Q₁ = 15, Q₂ = 7.5 (Cournot 10, 10)
> Stackelberg point on firm 2's reaction curve [chart:cournot]

@exam 78 | Kinked demand curve (Sweezy)
- Rivals match price cuts but not price rises
- Demand is elastic above the kink and inelastic below it
- MR has a vertical gap at Q*
- MC can move within the gap without changing P* or Q*: price rigidity
- Weakness: it does not explain how P* is set
> Kinked demand, broken MR and two MC curves through the gap [chart:kinked]

@exam 79 | Dominant firm and competitive fringe
- Residual demand = market demand − fringe supply
- The dominant firm sets MR_D = MC_D and charges P from residual demand
- The fringe takes that price and supplies where P = MC
- Fringe entry shrinks residual demand and erodes the dominant firm's power
= D_D(P) = D(P) − S_F(P)

@exam 80 | Cartels: OPEC vs CIPEC
- Need a stable organisation (agreement, no cheating) and potential monopoly power
- Power depends on inelastic market demand and inelastic non-member supply
- OPEC: inelastic oil demand and non-OPEC supply — large price increases
- CIPEC: elastic copper demand, scrap and outside supply — little power
- Each member is tempted to cheat because P > its MC at the cartel output

@exam 81 | Dominant strategies and Nash equilibrium
- Dominant strategy: best whatever the rival does
- Nash equilibrium: each player's choice is a best response to the others'
- Every dominant-strategy equilibrium is Nash, not vice versa
- Example matrix: advertising game where both advertise
> 2×2 payoff matrix: (Adv, Adv) 10,5; (Adv, Don't) 15,0; (Don't, Adv) 6,8; (Don't, Don't) 10,2

@exam 82 | Prisoners' dilemma in trade policy
- Protection is each country's dominant strategy
- (Protect, Protect) is the Nash equilibrium, worse for both than free trade
- Cooperation needs enforceable agreements (WTO), reciprocity, repeated interaction
> 2×2 matrix, e.g. (Free, Free) 10,10; (Protect, Free) 20,−5; (Free, Protect) −5,20; (Protect, Protect) 0,0

@exam 83 | Repeated games and tit-for-tat
- Tit-for-tat: cooperate first, then copy the rival's last move
- Infinite (or uncertain) repetition: the future punishment outweighs the one-off gain from cheating
- Needs a low discount rate, quick detection, credible punishment, few players
- With a known last round, cooperation unravels by backward induction

@exam 84 | Sequential games and backward induction
- Extensive form (game tree) shows the order of moves
- Backward induction: solve the last move first and work back
- Subgame perfect equilibrium: Nash in every subgame; rules out empty threats
- Moving first can be an advantage (product-choice game)
> Game tree with two stages and payoffs at the end nodes

@exam 85 | Credible vs empty threats; commitment
- A threat is credible only if carrying it out is in the threatener's interest
- Irreversible investments change your own payoffs and make threats credible
- DuPont built titanium dioxide capacity ahead of demand to deter entry

@exam 86 | Entry deterrence with extra capacity
- Stage 1: incumbent decides on capacity; stage 2: entrant decides; then pricing
- Extra capacity makes price war the best response after entry, so the threat is credible
- Deterrence pays if the capacity cost is below the profit it protects
- Otherwise accommodation is the profit-maximising strategy

@exam 87 | Brander–Spencer strategic trade policy (Boeing vs Airbus)
- Either firm alone is profitable; if both produce, both lose
- Two Nash equilibria: whoever moves first wins
- A European subsidy makes producing Airbus's dominant strategy, so Boeing stays out

@exam 88 | Auctions and the winner's curse

- Common value: item worth the same to all, but estimates differ (oil tracts)

@exam 89 | Derived demand; MRP vs VMP

@exam 90 | Hiring rule MRP = w

@exam 96 | Bilateral monopoly

@exam 97 | Stocks and flows

@exam 98 | Present discounted value

@exam 104 | Hotelling's rule and user cost
- A resource in the ground is an asset: its net price must rise at the rate of interest
- Otherwise producers shift extraction earlier or later

- In practice user cost is small; discoveries, technology and market power dominate prices

@exam 106 | Edgeworth box and the contract curve

@exam 107 | First welfare theorem

@exam 119 | Public goods
