@level 4 | factory-floor | The Factory Floor | Production functions & returns to scale | 25-32
@brief Production function q = F(K, L): the most output that each input combination can give, with today's technology.
@brief Short run: at least one input (usually capital) is fixed. Long run: every input can change. The length depends on the industry.
@brief MP > AP pulls the average up, MP < AP pulls it down, so MP cuts AP at AP's maximum.
@brief Diminishing marginal returns: with other inputs fixed, extra labour eventually adds less output. Output still rises, just more slowly.
@brief Isoquant slope = MRTS = MPL/MPK. Returns to scale ask what happens when ALL inputs grow together.

### single | 25 | 1
Q: What does a production function show?
* The maximum output from each combination of inputs, given the technology
- The cheapest way to produce a given level of output at current input prices
- The total revenue a firm earns from each level of output it sells
- The combinations of two goods an economy can produce with all its resources
E: q = F(K, L) is a technical relationship: it says nothing about prices or costs. Cost minimisation comes later, when input prices are added. The last option describes a production possibilities frontier.

### single | 25 | 2
Q: What separates the short run from the long run in production theory?
* Whether at least one input is fixed
- Whether the period is shorter or longer than one year
- Whether the firm is making a profit or a loss
- Whether output prices are fixed or flexible during the period
E: The short run is any period in which one or more inputs cannot be changed. The long run is long enough to vary all inputs. There is no fixed calendar length: for a coffee shop it may be weeks, for a power plant a decade.

### single | 25 | 2
Q: Building a new semiconductor fab takes about three years. For a chipmaker, what does this imply?
* Over a one-year horizon its fab capacity is a fixed input
- Its capital is variable as soon as the board approves a new fab
- Chipmakers have no short run, because they always plan years ahead
- Capital is fixed only if the firm is unprofitable
E: An input is fixed when it cannot be changed within the planning horizon. As the horizon lengthens past the construction time, capacity becomes variable — that is how the short run turns into the long run.

### single | 26 | 2
Q: Why must the marginal product curve cut the average product curve at the maximum of AP?
* If MP > AP the average rises, if MP < AP it falls, so AP peaks where MP = AP
- Because both curves are derived from the same isoquant and so must touch at its midpoint
- Because MP is always twice as large as AP
- Because total product is at its maximum where they cross
E: It is like grades: a new score above your average raises it, a score below lowers it. So while MP is above AP, AP rises; once MP falls below AP, AP falls. The turning point is where they are equal.

### single | 26 | 2
Q: On a total product curve, how can you read AP and MP graphically?

- AP is the slope of the curve itself, while MP is the slope of a ray from the origin to the point
- Both are measured by the area under the total product curve
E: AP = q/L is rise over run from the origin, so it is the slope of a ray. MP = Δq/ΔL is the slope of the tangent. AP is greatest where a ray from the origin is just tangent to the TP curve — exactly where AP = MP.

Q: What does the law of diminishing marginal returns say?

- Doubling all inputs eventually yields less than double the output, whatever the technology
- Total output eventually falls as more labour is hired
- The marginal product of labour falls from the very first worker
E: The law is about adding one input to fixed amounts of the others, such as more workers in the same factory. It is not about returns to scale, where all inputs grow. MP often rises at first, before diminishing returns set in.

- A curve showing the combinations of goods that give equal utility
E: "Iso-quant" means "same quantity". The line of equal cost is the isocost. A family of isoquants (the isoquant map) describes the whole production function, just as an indifference map describes preferences.

- Because returns to scale are always increasing
- Because firms always use equal amounts of capital and labour
E: As the firm replaces capital with more and more labour, each extra worker has less capital to work with and adds less output. So it takes ever more labour to replace each unit of capital, and the isoquant flattens.

- The firm has found its cost-minimising input mix
E: If more labour must be matched with more capital to keep output constant, the extra labour must be lowering output (negative MP). A firm could produce the same output with less of both inputs, so this "uneconomic region" is never chosen.

- Because the isocost line has slope w/r
- Because MRTS is defined as total output divided by total labour
E: Moving along an isoquant, the output gained from extra labour must exactly offset the output lost from less capital. Rearranging gives the slope of the isoquant as the ratio of marginal products.

- The firm can produce only one level of output

@level 5 | counting-costs | Counting Costs | Economic cost, short & long run | 33-40
@brief Economic cost = opportunity cost: it includes implicit costs, such as the owner's time, the firm's own building and the owner's capital.
@brief Sunk costs cannot be recovered, so they must not affect forward-looking decisions.
@brief TC = FC + VC. MC = ΔTC/Δq = ΔVC/Δq. MC cuts AVC and ATC at their minimum points.
@brief Cost minimisation: MRTS = w/r, or MPL/w = MPK/r — the last dollar spent on each input yields the same output.
@brief LAC is the envelope of SAC curves. Economies of scope: joint production is cheaper. Learning curve: cost falls with cumulative output.

E: Accountants record explicit outlays and depreciation for financial statements. Economists ask what resources could earn in their best alternative use, so implicit costs such as forgone rent or the owner's forgone salary count too.
