@level 1 | market-forces | Market Forces | Supply, demand & elasticity | 1-8
@brief Price moves toward equilibrium: excess demand bids it up, excess supply pushes it down, until Qd = Qs.
@brief A change in the good's own price moves you ALONG a curve. Anything else (costs, technology, incomes, other prices) SHIFTS the curve.
@brief Elasticity = % change in quantity ÷ % change in price. It is not the slope: on a straight demand line it changes at every point.
@brief Time matters: demand for nondurables and supply of commodities are more elastic in the long run; demand for durables is more elastic in the short run.
@brief A binding price ceiling turns a temporary excess demand into a lasting shortage, so something other than price does the rationing.

### single | 1 | 1
Q: The market price of copper is above its equilibrium level. What brings it back to equilibrium?
* Excess supply builds up, so sellers cut prices until Qd = Qs
- Buyers bid prices higher because they fear the surplus will soon run out
- The demand curve shifts right until it crosses supply at the current price
- Producers raise output until the extra supply clears at the higher price
E: Above equilibrium Qs > Qd, so there is excess supply. Competition among sellers pushes the price down, which raises Qd and lowers Qs until they meet. Nothing shifts — the adjustment is a movement along both curves.

### single | 1 | 2
Q: A strike unexpectedly closes several large copper mines. In a competitive world market, what happens?
* Supply shifts left; at the old price there is excess demand, so price rises and quantity falls
- Demand shifts right because buyers stock up, so both price and quantity rise
- Supply moves along the curve to a lower quantity while the price stays the same
- Supply shifts left, but the price stays put because a world market absorbs the shock
E: Lost mine output is a supply shift, not a price-driven movement. At the old price buyers want more than is available; the excess demand is temporary because the price rises until the market clears at a higher P and lower Q.

### single | 1,8 | 2
Q: What separates the excess demand after a supply disruption from the shortage caused by a binding price ceiling?
* With a ceiling the price is not allowed to rise, so the gap between Qd and Qs persists
- After a disruption the gap is larger, so it takes longer for the market to clear
- A ceiling raises quantity supplied, while a disruption reduces it
- They are the same thing: both last until the government ends the crisis
E: In a free market, excess demand is self-correcting — the price rises and closes the gap. A legal maximum price blocks that mechanism, so the shortage lasts as long as the ceiling does.

- (ΔP/P) ÷ (ΔQ/Q)

### single | 3 | 2
Q: Why is price elasticity not the same thing as the slope of a straight-line demand curve?
* E = (ΔQ/ΔP)·(P/Q): the slope term is constant, but the ratio P/Q changes along the line
- The slope uses percentages, elasticity uses absolute units
- Elasticity is constant along a straight line, while the slope keeps changing
- They are the same, measured in different currencies
E: On a linear demand curve ΔQ/ΔP never changes, but P/Q is high near the price axis and low near the quantity axis. So demand is elastic at high prices and inelastic at low prices, even though the slope is the same everywhere.

### single | 3 | 2
Q: Why do economists use the arc (midpoint) elasticity when measuring elasticity between two price points?
* Using average P and Q gives the same answer whether the price rises or falls
- It measures elasticity at a single point, which is more precise
- It is the only formula that works for curved demand curves
- It always gives a value closer to −1 than point elasticity does
E: With the simple formula, going from P=4 to P=5 gives a different percentage change than going from 5 to 4. The arc formula uses the midpoint of P and Q, so the result is symmetric. Point elasticity is the version used at one point on the curve.

### single | 3 | 2

- Where the curve meets the price axis
- At the lowest price, where the most units are sold
- Where demand is most inelastic
E: Above the midpoint demand is elastic, so cutting the price raises spending. Below it demand is inelastic, so cutting the price lowers spending. Spending peaks where |E| = 1, which on a straight line is exactly halfway.

@level 2 | taste-test | Taste Test | Preferences, budgets & optimal choice | 9-16
@brief Four assumptions: completeness, transitivity, more is better, diminishing MRS (convex indifference curves).
@brief MRS = how much Y you would give up for one more X and stay equally happy = |slope of the indifference curve| = MUx/MUy.
@brief Budget line: I = Px·X + Py·Y, slope −Px/Py. Income changes shift it; price changes rotate it.
@brief Interior optimum: MRS = Px/Py, or MUx/Px = MUy/Py. If no tangency exists, you get a corner solution.
@brief Choices reveal preferences (WARP). Laspeyres overstates cost-of-living increases; Paasche understates them.

### multi | 9 | 1
Q: Which are standard assumptions about consumer preferences in Pindyck & Rubinfeld?
* Completeness — the consumer can rank any two bundles
* Transitivity — if X beats Y and Y beats Z, then X beats Z
* More is better — goods are desirable
* Diminishing MRS — indifference curves are convex
- Cardinality — utility can be measured in fixed units
E: Consumer theory needs only an ordinal ranking. Utility numbers just order bundles; their size has no meaning. The four listed assumptions give downward-sloping, non-crossing, convex indifference curves.

### single | 9 | 2
Q: Why can two indifference curves never cross?
* One of two equally good bundles would contain more of both goods
- Because the budget line can only be tangent to one indifference curve at a time
- Because indifference curves must be parallel by definition
- Because utility cannot be measured, so curves cannot meet
E: If curves U₁ and U₂ crossed at A, then A ~ B (on U₁) and A ~ D (on U₂), so by transitivity B ~ D. But one of B or D contains more of both goods, so by "more is better" it must be preferred. That contradiction rules out crossing.

### single | 9 | 1

- More is better
- Diminishing MRS
E: Transitivity requires that if tea beats coffee and coffee beats juice, tea must beat juice. Circular rankings make a consistent "best" choice impossible.

- Because MRS is defined as the ratio of total utilities

### single | 10 | 2

- The consumer will always give up the same amount of Y for each extra X

@level 3 | demand-unpacked | Demand Unpacked | Income & substitution effects, market demand | 17-24
@brief Price-consumption curve (PCC): optimal bundles as Px changes → read off the individual demand curve.
@brief Income-consumption curve (ICC): optimal bundles as income changes → Engel curves (necessity 0 < E < 1, luxury E > 1, inferior E < 0).
@brief A price change = substitution effect (always opposite to price) + income effect (depends on normal vs inferior).
@brief Giffen good: inferior AND the income effect outweighs the substitution effect, so demand slopes up. Very rare.
@brief Market demand = horizontal sum of individual demands. Network effects make demand more elastic (bandwagon) or less elastic (snob).

### single | 17 | 2
Q: How is an individual demand curve derived from indifference curves?
* Vary Px, find each tangency (the PCC) and plot Px against the chosen X
- Change income with prices fixed and plot income against the chosen X
- Add up the quantities chosen by all consumers at each price of the good
- Plot the marginal utility of X against the quantity of X
E: Each price of X gives a new budget line and a new optimum. Linking these optima traces the PCC; plotting each price against the quantity chosen gives the demand curve. Varying income instead traces the ICC and Engel curve.

* With convex curves, a flatter budget line touches the same curve at more X
- Because all goods are normal goods
- Because consumers always buy more when they feel richer
- Because the budget line always shifts outward after the price of a good falls

Q: Put the steps of decomposing a fall in Px (Hicksian method) in order.
1. Find the original optimum on U₁ with the initial budget line
2. Rotate the budget line for the lower Px and find the new optimum on U₂
3. Draw a hypothetical line parallel to the new budget line but tangent to U₁
4. Substitution effect = move along U₁ to that hypothetical tangency
5. Income effect = move from the hypothetical tangency to the new optimum
E: The hypothetical budget line has the new price ratio but just enough income to keep the consumer on the old curve. That isolates the pure effect of relative prices. The parallel shift from that line to the actual new line is the income effect.

E: All Giffen goods are inferior, but in most inferior goods the substitution effect still dominates, so demand slopes down. Only when the opposing income effect is larger does a price rise increase quantity demanded.

* Only then does the income effect work against the substitution effect
- Because Giffen goods are bought only by people with low incomes
- Because a Giffen good's substitution effect is positive
- Because inferior goods always have upward-sloping demand curves in practice
E: The substitution effect always pushes quantity opposite to price. For demand to slope upward, something must push the other way — and only the income effect of an inferior good does that. For a normal good both effects reinforce each other.

* A staple food takes a large share of a poor household's budget
- A rich household buys luxury goods to signal status
- A firm buys inputs whose price is regulated

Q: How is market demand obtained from individual demand curves?
* Add the quantities demanded by all buyers at each price (horizontal summation)
- Add the prices each buyer would pay for each quantity (vertical summation)
- Take the average of the individual demand curves

E: For private goods each unit is consumed by one person, so at any price market quantity is the sum of individual quantities. Vertical summation is used for public goods, where everyone consumes the same units.

Q: Why can a market demand curve have kinks?
* Each buyer starts purchasing at a different price
- Because buyers have kinked indifference curves
- Because sellers change prices in steps
- Because horizontal summation is only an approximation
E: Above a buyer's maximum price, that buyer demands nothing. As price falls past each such choke price, a new buyer's quantities are added and the market curve becomes flatter at that point.

Q: What is consumer surplus?
* Willingness to pay minus the price actually paid, summed over units bought
- The extra quantity consumers would buy if the price fell
- The revenue firms earn above their costs
- The area between the supply curve and the market price, summed over all units
E: Each unit's value to the buyer is the height of the demand curve. Subtracting the price paid and adding across all units bought gives the triangle-shaped area between demand and price. The area above supply is producer surplus.

* A price cut draws users in, which makes the platform worth more to others
- Users stop joining when many others are already on the platform

Q: What is speculative demand?
* Demand driven by expected price increases rather than by use
- Demand from consumers who buy only when prices are low, such as bargain hunters
- Demand that depends on the price of a complement
- Demand for goods bought on credit
E: Speculators buy now to resell later or to avoid paying more later. That is why a rising price can increase current purchases — buyers expect further increases.

* Rising prices raise expected future prices, shifting demand right
- Housing is a Giffen good, so its demand curve slopes upward
- Rising prices make houses more useful to live in
