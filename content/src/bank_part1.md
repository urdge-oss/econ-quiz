@level 1 | basics | The Big Picture | World economy: concept, history, structure, country groups | 1-5
@brief The world economy is a system of national economies linked by the international division of labour.
@brief The world market (exchange) appeared first, in the 16th–18th centuries; the world economy took shape by the end of the 19th century.
@brief World output was about $115 trillion in 2025; growth 3.5% (IMF). The US (~26%) and China (~17%) produce over 40% of it.
@brief Size (potential) = total GDP, population, resources. Level of development = GDP per capita, HDI, productivity, share of services.
@brief Main groups: advanced economies, developing countries, economies in transition, NICs, least developed countries.

### single | 1 | 1
Q: What is the key difference between the world MARKET and the world ECONOMY?
* The world market covers only exchange; the world economy also includes production, distribution and consumption
- The world market includes services, while the world economy includes only goods
- The world economy appeared first, in the 15th century, and the world market grew out of it only in the 20th century
- The world market is regulated by the WTO, while the world economy has no regulation
E: The world market (16th–18th c.) is the sphere of exchange based on the international division of labour. The world economy (formed by the end of the 19th c.) is the wider system: production + exchange + distribution + consumption.

### single | 1 | 1
Q: What links national economies into one world economy?
* The international division of labour
- A single world currency issued by the IMF
- A common world government with binding laws
- Equal levels of economic development everywhere
E: Countries specialize and exchange — that is the international division of labour (IDL). It creates flows of goods, services, capital, labour and technology between them.

### multi | 1 | 2
Q: Which of these are SUBJECTS (actors) of the world economy? Select all that apply.
* Multinational enterprises
* International organisations such as the IMF and WTO
- The international division of labour
- Gold reserves
- Exchange rates
E: Subjects are actors that make decisions: MNEs, states, international organisations, regional blocs, banks, households. The division of labour is the basis of the world economy, and gold reserves are an instrument — neither is an actor.

- 1945–1971
- 1991–2008
E: Between the 1870s and WWI the gold standard, capital exports, railways and steamships created a highly integrated world economy. WWI and the Great Depression then broke it apart.

E: Two world wars and the 1929–1933 Depression led to high tariffs, competitive devaluations and closed currency blocs. GATT and liberalization came only after 1945.

@level 2 | trade | Trade Winds | International trade: factors, division of labour, indicators, policy | 6-12
@brief Factors of production: land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship; today also technology, knowledge and data.
@brief Comparative advantage (Ricardo): specialize where your relative cost is lowest. Heckscher–Ohlin: export goods that use your abundant factor.
@brief Forms of IDL: general → particular → single (detailed); today global value chains.
@brief Key indicators: X − M (balance), X + M (turnover), X/GDP (export quota), export prices ÷ import prices × 100 (terms of trade).
@brief World trade in goods and services passed $35 trillion in 2025; top goods exporters: China, US, Germany.
@brief Trade policy tools: tariffs (ad valorem, specific) and non-tariff measures (quotas, licences, standards, subsidies).

### single | 6 | 1
Q: Which set lists the classical factors of production?
* Land, labour, capital
- Money, prices, profit
- Exports, imports, tariffs
- Supply, demand, equilibrium
E: Classical economists named land (natural resources), labour and capital; entrepreneurship was added later. Today knowledge, technology and data are also seen as factors.

### single | 6 | 2
Q: According to M. Porter, which of these is an ADVANCED factor rather than a basic one?
* Highly skilled engineers and research institutes
- Large oil and natural gas deposits
- A warm climate with fertile farmland
- Abundant cheap and unskilled labour moving from rural regions to cities
E: Basic factors are inherited (resources, climate, unskilled labour). Advanced factors are created by investment — skills, R&D, digital infrastructure — and give lasting competitive advantage.

### single | 6 | 2
Q: The Heckscher–Ohlin theory says a country will export goods that…
* Intensively use the factor of production it has in abundance
- It produces with the highest absolute productivity compared with all its trading partners
- Have the highest world prices
- Its government subsidizes the most
E: H–O: labour-abundant countries export labour-intensive goods, capital-abundant countries export capital-intensive goods. Absolute productivity is A. Smith's idea.

### single | 6 | 3
Q: What did the Leontief paradox show?
* US exports were more labour-intensive than its imports, although the US was capital-abundant
- Resource-rich countries grew more slowly than countries without natural resources
- Free trade lowered real wages in capital-abundant countries such as the US
- Trade between countries with similar factor endowments was close to zero in the post-war period
E: In 1953 W. Leontief found that US exports used relatively more labour than imports — contrary to Heckscher–Ohlin. One explanation: American labour is highly skilled (human capital).

### single | 7 | 1
Q: Who formulated the theory of comparative advantage?
* David Ricardo
- Adam Smith
- John Maynard Keynes
- Michael Porter
E: Ricardo (1817): a country gains from trade by specializing where its relative (opportunity) cost is lowest, even if it is worse at producing everything. Smith's idea was absolute advantage.

### single | 7 | 2
Q: A country produces both wine and cloth less efficiently than its partner, but its disadvantage is smallest in wine. According to Ricardo, it should…
* Specialize in wine and import cloth
- Produce both goods and not trade
- Specialize in cloth because its disadvantage there is larger
- Import both goods
E: Comparative advantage depends on relative costs. The country should specialize where its disadvantage is smallest (lowest opportunity cost) — then both partners gain.

### order | 7 | 2
Q: Order the forms of the international division of labour from the broadest to the most detailed.
1. General — between large spheres (industry vs agriculture)
2. Particular — between types of finished products within an industry
3. Single (detailed) — between components and production stages
E: General IDL divides spheres, particular IDL divides products, single IDL divides parts and stages — the basis of global value chains.

### single | 7 | 2
Q: Germany exports cars to France and imports French cars. This is an example of…
* Intra-industry trade
- Inter-industry trade
- Barter
- Dumping
E: Intra-industry trade = exchange of similar products (cars for cars), typical for developed countries with similar factor endowments. Inter-industry trade = different products (oil for machines).

### single | 7 | 3
Q: A product makes up 10% of a country's exports but only 2% of world exports. What is its RCA (Balassa) index and what does it mean?
* 5 — the country specializes in this product
- 0.2 — the country has no comparative advantage in this product
- 8 — the country imports this product
- 12 — the product is a re-export
E: RCA = share in the country's exports ÷ share in world exports = 10% ÷ 2% = 5. RCA above 1 indicates a revealed comparative advantage (specialization).

### single | 7 | 2
Q: Design in the US, chips from Taiwan, assembly in China or India. This is an example of…
* A global value chain
- Import substitution
- A customs union
- A currency board
E: In global value chains production is split into stages located in different countries, usually coordinated by an MNE. It is the modern form of the detailed division of labour.

### single | 8 | 2

### order | 8 | 2

@brief Embodied technology comes inside machines; disembodied technology is knowledge: patents, licences, know-how.

- Maturity
- Decline
