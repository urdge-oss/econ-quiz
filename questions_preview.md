## Level 1 · The Big Picture — World economy: concept, history, structure, country groups
Exam questions 1–5 · 18 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- The world economy is a system of national economies linked by the international division of labour.
- The world market (exchange) appeared first, in the 16th–18th centuries; the world economy took shape by the end of the 19th century.
- World output was about $115 trillion in 2025; growth 3.5% (IMF). The US (~26%) and China (~17%) produce over 40% of it.
- Size (potential) = total GDP, population, resources. Level of development = GDP per capita, HDI, productivity, share of services.
- Main groups: advanced economies, developing countries, economies in transition, NICs, least developed countries.

- ✅ The world market covers only exchange; the world economy also includes production, distribution and consumption

- ▫️ The world market is regulated by the WTO, while the world economy has no regulation
- ▫️ The world economy appeared first, in the 15th century, and the world market grew out of it only in the 20th century

> The world market (16th–18th c.) is the sphere of exchange based on the international division of labour. The world economy (formed by the end of the 19th c.) is the wider system: production + exchange + distribution + consumption.

## Level 2 · Trade Winds — International trade: factors, division of labour, indicators, policy
Exam questions 6–12 · 27 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- Factors of production: land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship; today also technology, knowledge and data.
- Comparative advantage (Ricardo): specialize where your relative cost is lowest. Heckscher–Ohlin: export goods that use your abundant factor.
- Forms of IDL: general → particular → single (detailed); today global value chains.
- Key indicators: X − M (balance), X + M (turnover), X/GDP (export quota), export prices ÷ import prices × 100 (terms of trade).
- World trade in goods and services passed $35 trillion in 2025; top goods exporters: China, US, Germany.
- Trade policy tools: tariffs (ad valorem, specific) and non-tariff measures (quotas, licences, standards, subsidies).

> Classical economists named land (natural resources), labour and capital; entrepreneurship was added later. Today knowledge, technology and data are also seen as factors.

- ▫️ Its government subsidizes the most

## Level 3 · Know-How — Technology exchange, life cycle, transfer, licences
Exam questions 13–19 · 23 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- Embodied technology comes inside machines; disembodied technology is knowledge: patents, licences, know-how.
- Commercial forms of exchange are paid (licences, engineering, franchising); non-commercial are free (publications, conferences, academic exchange).
- Technology life cycle: emergence → introduction → growth → maturity → decline. A licence is most expensive at the growth stage.
- The largest channel of technology transfer is inside multinational enterprises.
- Licences: simple (non-exclusive), exclusive, full; payments: royalties, lump sum or combined.
- Global Innovation Index 2026: Switzerland, Sweden, US, South Korea, Singapore lead; China is 10th.

- ✅ Knowledge transferred without equipment — patents, licences, know-how

> Embodied technology comes inside goods (machines, materials). Disembodied technology is pure knowledge: patents, licences, know-how, software, consulting.

## Level 4 · Follow the Capital — Investment, FDI and portfolio flows
Exam questions 20–27 · 22 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- Global FDI rose 6% to about $1.6 trillion in 2025; the US was both the largest recipient ($277 bn) and the largest source.

## Level 5 · Money Matters — World finance and monetary systems
Exam questions 28–37 · 30 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- Finance globalized through liberalization, ICT, floating exchange rates and financial innovation.

## Level 6 · Giants Without Borders — Transnational corporations and Dunning's OLI paradigm
Exam questions 38–48 · 26 quiz questions

**Briefing**

- MNEs produce about one-third of world GDP and more than half of world exports (OECD).
- Transnationality Index (UNCTAD) = average of foreign shares in assets, sales and employment.
- Dunning's OLI: Ownership (what the firm has), Location (where to produce), Internalization (how to use the advantage).

- Strategic alliances keep firms legally independent; mergers and acquisitions do not.

## Exam questions — key points (oral practice mode)

**1. The concept of world economy and world market**
- World economy: system of national economies linked by the international division of labour
- Subjects: states, MNEs, international organisations, regional blocs, banks, households
- World market = exchange; world economy = production, exchange, distribution, consumption
- The world market came first (16th–18th c.); the world economy formed by the end of the 19th c.

**2. The main stages of formation and development of the world economy**
- 16th–18th c.: Great Discoveries, colonies, first world market
- Industrial Revolution; 1870s–1914 "first globalization"; 1914–1945 disintegration
- 1945–1991 bipolar world: Bretton Woods, GATT, Marshall Plan, end of colonial system
- 1990s–2007 globalization (WTO, euro, China in WTO); after 2008 "slowbalization" and fragmentation

**3. The structure of the world economy at the present stage**
- By development level: advanced economies vs emerging and developing economies
- By sectors: services about two-thirds of world output
- By regions: North America, Europe, East Asia; India rising (4th economy in 2025)
- Functional: trade, capital, labour, technology, currency relations; institutions: IMF, WB, WTO, G20

**4. Main indicators of the level of economic development and economic potential of the country**
- Potential (size): GDP, GNI, population, resources, capital, R&D, share in world exports
- Level: GDP/GNI per capita, HDI, productivity, sectoral structure, Gini index
- World Bank FY2027: low ≤ $1,175; high > $14,375 GNI per capita
- Example: China — huge potential, middle level; US — both large

**5. The main groups of countries that are part of the world economy**
- Developed (advanced) economies — about 40 countries
- Developing countries; countries in transition (post-socialist)
- NICs and emerging markets; least developed countries (UN list)
- Other groupings: G7, G20, OECD, BRICS, OPEC; World Bank income groups

**6. Concepts and main types of factors of production**
- Land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship; today also technology, knowledge, data
- Basic vs advanced factors (Porter); general vs specialized; mobile vs immobile
- Heckscher–Ohlin: export goods that use the abundant factor; Leontief paradox
- Trend: knowledge and AI become the decisive factor

**7. The essence and main forms of international division of labour**
- IDL: specialization of countries in goods and services for exchange
- Theories: absolute (Smith), comparative advantage (Ricardo), Heckscher–Ohlin
- Forms: general, particular, single (detailed); inter-, intra-industry, intra-firm
- Modern form: global value chains; RCA index above 1 = specialization

**8. The World Market: essence and evolution**
- System of stable trade links between national markets based on the IDL
- Segments: goods, services, capital, labour, technology, currency
- Evolution: local → international → world market (16th–18th c.) → global market (WTO, e-commerce)
- Size: trade in goods and services above $35 trn in 2025

**9. The concept and main indicators of international trade**
- Exports, imports, turnover (X + M), trade balance (X − M)
- Export quota = X/GDP; foreign trade quota measures openness
- Terms of trade = export price index ÷ import price index × 100
- Germany exports ~40% of GDP, the US ~11%

**10. The commodity and geographical structure of world trade at the present stage**
- Goods about three-quarters, services one-quarter and growing faster
- Manufactures dominate; electronics and AI goods drove 2025 growth
- Asia gave 71% of merchandise volume growth in 2025
- Top exporters: China $3.77 trn, US $2.19 trn, Germany $1.76 trn

**11. The dynamics of international trade at the present stage**
- 1950–2008 trade grew faster than GDP; after 2008 "slowbalization"
- 2020 COVID collapse, then rebound
- 2025: goods volume +4.6% despite US tariffs (frontloading, AI goods)
- 2026 WTO forecast: +3.9%; risks — tariffs, fragmentation, conflicts

**12. Foreign trade policy: basic directions and tools**
- Free trade vs protectionism; import substitution vs export orientation
- Tariffs: ad valorem, specific, combined
- Non-tariff: quotas, licences, standards, subsidies, anti-dumping duties, sanctions
- WTO principles: MFN, national treatment; 2025 US tariffs, struck down in part in February 2026

**13. Concepts and main forms of international scientific and technical exchange**
- Embodied (in machines) vs disembodied (patents, licences, know-how) technology
- Commercial forms: licences, engineering, franchising, high-tech goods, FDI
- Non-commercial: publications, conferences, academic exchange, migration of specialists
- Joint research programmes: CERN, ISS, Horizon Europe

**14. The main stages of international scientific and technical exchange**
- Before the 19th c.: spread through crafts and migration
- 19th c.: export of machines, Paris Convention (1883)
- 1950s–1970s STR: boom of licence trade (Japan's catch-up)
- 1980s–1990s ICT and TRIPS (1994); 21st c.: digital, open innovation, export controls on chips

**15. The stages of the technology life cycle. The life cycle and directions of technology movement**
- Emergence → introduction → growth → maturity → decline (S-curve)
- A licence is most expensive at the growth stage
- Mature technologies move from leaders to NICs and then to developing countries
- Vernon's product life cycle; Akamatsu's "flying geese"

**16. The geographical spread of the achievements of the STR (NTR)**
- Concentrated in the US, EU, Japan, South Korea and increasingly China
- Spread through trade, FDI, licences, migration → technological gap
- GII 2026 top: Switzerland, Sweden, US, Korea, Singapore; China 10th
- Fourth Industrial Revolution: AI, robotics, IoT, biotech

**17. Organizational forms of technology transfer**
- Licence agreements, sale of patents, franchising
- Engineering and turnkey contracts
- FDI and joint ventures — intra-firm transfer is the largest channel
- Industrial cooperation, R&D alliances, trade in equipment, training

**18. License agreements. Royalties**
- Licensor grants the licensee the right to use IP for a fee
- Simple (non-exclusive), exclusive, full; also cross-licence, compulsory licence
- Payments: royalties (% of sales), lump sum, combined
- Protection: Paris Convention, WIPO, TRIPS; patent ~20 years

**19. Classification of countries by participation in technological exchange**
- Leaders and net exporters: US, Japan, Germany, UK, Switzerland, Korea
- Exporters and importers: France, Netherlands, Sweden, Israel, China (rising)
- Importers-adapters: India, Brazil, Mexico, Malaysia, Turkey
- Technologically dependent: least developed countries

**20. Investments and international investments**
- Investment: long-term placing of capital for income; real vs financial
- International investment: cross-border capital for income or control
- Motives: returns, markets, resources, tariff jumping, diversification, taxes
- Global FDI $1.6 trn in 2025 (+6%); concentrated in top 20 hosts

**21. Main groups of investors**
- Private: MNEs, banks, individuals
- Institutional: pension funds, insurers, mutual and hedge funds
- State: governments, central banks, sovereign wealth funds
- International organisations: World Bank, IMF, EBRD, EIB

**22. The main forms of international capital movement**
- Private vs official; short-term vs long-term
- Loan capital (interest) vs entrepreneurial capital (profit)
- BPM6: direct, portfolio, derivatives, other investment, reserves
- Also aid (ODA) and migrant remittances

**23. Types of foreign direct investment. The main sources of foreign direct investment**
- FDI = 10% or more of votes; lasting interest and control
- Horizontal, vertical (backward, forward), conglomerate
- Motives (Dunning): market-, resource-, efficiency-, strategic-asset-seeking
- Sources: US No. 1 (2025), Japan, China, Germany, UK; components — equity, reinvested earnings, intra-company loans

**24. Organizational forms of foreign direct investment**
- Greenfield vs brownfield (M&A) — e.g., Nippon Steel bought US Steel ($13.8 bn)
- Subsidiary (> 50%), associate (10–50%), branch
- Joint ventures, reinvestment of profits
- Special purpose entities in conduit economies

**25. Quantitative criteria for foreign direct investment**
- Threshold 10% of voting power (IMF BPM6, OECD Benchmark)
- Subsidiary > 50%, associate 10–50%, branch 100%
- Flows vs stock; FDI income; FDI/GDP
- 2025: developed economies $723 bn (+11%), developing $901 bn (+2%)

**26. FDI growth waves**
- 1870s–1914 British wave; 1950s–60s US multinationals in Europe
- Late 1980s Japanese wave; around 2000 M&A boom; 2007 peak
- 2015–16 peak; 2020 COVID fall; 2025 +6%
- Waves start with technology and liberalization, end with crises

**27. Reasons for growth and decrease in portfolio investment**
- Portfolio = shares below 10% and bonds; "hot money"
- Growth: liberalization, low rates abroad, diversification, pension funds, ETFs
- Decrease: crises, higher US rates, sanctions, devaluations, downgrades
- Risk of sudden stops (Asia 1997, Russia 1998); AI boom pulls capital to US tech

**28. Factors of globalization of world finances**
- Liberalization and deregulation (London Big Bang, 1986)
- ICT, electronic trading, fintech
- Floating exchange rates after 1973; financial innovation
- FX turnover $9.6 trn a day (2025)

**29. Financial flows in the global world**
- FDI, portfolio investment, bank loans, official flows, remittances, derivatives
- Most flows are between developed countries; capital also flows "uphill" (Lucas paradox)
- US net international position about −$26 trn — largest net debtor
- Volatility and concentration in financial centres

**30. The main subjects of world finance**
- States and central banks (FX reserves $13.2 trn)
- IMF, World Bank, BIS, regional development banks, BRICS bank
- Commercial and investment banks, institutional investors, sovereign funds
- MNEs, exchanges, rating agencies, households

**31. Elements of world finance**
- International monetary system
- Global financial markets
- International financial institutions and regulators
- MNE finance, public international finance, financial infrastructure

**32. Global financial market: essence, elements and main features**
- Redistributes capital across borders through intermediaries
- Segments: FX, money, credit, securities (eurobonds, foreign bonds), derivatives, gold, insurance
- London has about 38% of FX trading
- Features: 24-hour trading, concentration, volatility, contagion, dollar dominance

**33. Gold standard**
- Fixed gold content, free convertibility, free gold flows (1870s–1914)
- Automatic adjustment: Hume's price–specie flow
- Forms: gold coin → gold bullion → gold exchange standard (Genoa 1922)
- Collapse: WWI, uneven gold, Great Depression (UK left 1931)

**34. The Bretton Woods System**
- 1944, 44 countries; created the IMF and IBRD
- $35 per ounce; currencies pegged to the dollar ±1%
- Crisis: Triffin dilemma, US deficits, gold outflow
- End: Nixon closed the gold window (1971); floating from 1973

**35. The Jamaican System**
- Kingston 1976, in force 1978
- Gold demonetized; SDR as main reserve asset; free choice of regime
- SDR basket: USD, EUR, CNY, JPY, GBP
- In practice the dollar dominates (56.7% of reserves, 2026); volatility and crises

**36. European Monetary System**
- Background: currency snake (1972); launched 13 March 1979
- ECU, ERM (±2.25%), European Monetary Cooperation Fund
- Crisis 1992–93: UK and Italy left; band widened to ±15%
- Paved the way to the euro (ECU → euro 1:1)

**37. European Monetary Union**
- Delors Report (1989): three stages; euro 1999, cash 2002
- Maastricht criteria: deficit ≤ 3%, debt ≤ 60%, inflation and rates close to best
- ECB and Eurosystem; 21 members since Bulgaria joined in 2026
- Weakness: no common fiscal policy; debt crisis 2010–12

**38. The concept and essence of transnationalization**
- Expansion of firms abroad through FDI under one control centre
- Stages: export → sales offices → production abroad → global network
- Measured by UNCTAD's Transnationality Index
- Foreign affiliate output is comparable to world trade

**39. Transnational corporations and their role in the global economy**
- MNE: headquarters in one country, affiliates in others
- About 1/3 of world GDP, over half of world exports (OECD)
- Main source of FDI, technology, value chains, R&D
- Tax avoidance → global minimum tax 15% (2024)

**40. "The Eclectic Paradigm" by J. Dunning**
- OLI: Ownership, Location, Internalization advantages
- O only → licensing; O + I → export; O + I + L → FDI
- Combines Hymer, internalization theory and location theory
- Extension: investment development path

**41. Ownership advantages (OLI)**
- Firm-specific assets that offset the "liability of foreignness"
- Oa: technology, brands, know-how, management
- Ot: benefits of managing a network (scale, scope, sourcing)
- Oi: institutional — culture, governance

**42. Internalization advantages (OLI)**
- Better to use the advantage inside the firm than sell it on the market
- Transaction cost theory (Coase, Williamson, Buckley & Casson)
- Reasons: protect know-how, control quality, avoid negotiation costs, transfer pricing
- Low market failures → licensing; high → FDI

**43. Location advantages (OLI)**
- Host-country features that make producing there better than exporting
- Resources, market size, costs, infrastructure, skills
- Trade barriers, taxes and incentives, institutions, clusters
- Examples: Mexico nearshoring, Ireland taxes, Vietnam labour

**44. Features of modern transnationalization**
- Global value chains and non-equity modes (contract manufacturing)
- MNEs from emerging markets; state-owned MNEs
- Digital "asset-light" MNEs
- Reshoring, nearshoring, friendshoring; FDI screening; minimum tax

**45. The global investment boom and its causes**
- Booms: 1990s–2000, 2003–07, AI boom 2024–26
- Causes: liberalization, privatization, ICT, cheap credit, integration, opening of China
- AI: US greenfield announcements ~$360 bn in 2025, over half in AI sectors
- Booms end with crises

**46. New features of TNCs**
- Network structure; transnational strategy (Bartlett & Ghoshal)
- Intangible assets, platforms, ecosystems
- New players: emerging-market and state-owned MNEs
- ESG, due diligence, supply-chain resilience

**47. Strategic alliances of TNCs**
- Long-term cooperation of independent firms
- Equity (joint ventures, stakes) and non-equity (R&D consortia, code-sharing)
- Motives: share costs and risks, access markets and technology, set standards
- Examples: Star Alliance, Renault–Nissan–Mitsubishi, Microsoft–OpenAI

**48. The influence of TNCs on the formation of global interdependence of countries**
- Tie economies together through value chains, FDI, technology, finance
- Shocks spread fast: 2021 chip shortage, COVID-19
- Host effects: capital, jobs, technology vs dependence, crowding out, tax avoidance
- States respond with FDI screening and "de-risking"

**49. Business cycle**
- Recurring rises and falls of activity around the trend
- Phases: expansion, peak, contraction, trough, recovery
- Output gap; investment swings most
- Causes: demand and supply shocks, credit cycles, technology, policy

**50. Recession. Common features of recessions**
- Two quarters of falling GDP (rule of thumb); NBER broader definition
- Global recession = fall in world GDP per capita
- Falling output, investment, profits, imports; rising unemployment and bankruptcies
- Usually about a year; financial crises cause deeper ones

**51. Macroeconomic equilibrium in the economy**
- Aggregate demand = aggregate supply
- Keynes: S = I; open economy I + G + X = S + T + M
- Short run vs long run (potential output)
- Inflationary and recessionary gaps; policy restores balance

**52. The main types of economic cycles**
- Kitchin 3–4 years (inventories)
- Juglar 7–11 years (equipment)
- Kuznets 15–25 years (construction, demography)
- Kondratiev 45–60 years (technological revolutions); political cycle (Nordhaus)

**53. Major world recessions. The main features of recessions over the past 20 years**
- Global recessions: 1975, 1982, 1991, 2009, 2020
- Recent: financial or non-economic origin, high synchronization
- Massive policy response: zero rates, QE, stimulus; rising debt
- 2009 world trade −12%; 2020 world GDP about −3%

**54. The 1974–1975 recession**
- OPEC embargo: oil ×4 (~$3 → ~$12)
- First world recession after WWII; synchronized
- Stagflation; end of the post-war "golden age"
- Energy saving, rise of monetarism, petrodollar recycling

**55. The 1980–1982 recession**
- Second oil shock (1979) and tight US money (Volcker, ~20%)
- US unemployment 10.8% (1982)
- Latin American debt crisis (Mexico, 1982)
- Victory over inflation; Reaganomics and Thatcherism

**56. The 1991–1993 recession**
- Gulf War oil spike, US savings-and-loan crisis
- Japan's bubble burst, German reunification, ERM crisis
- Unsynchronized: US 1990–91, Europe 1992–93
- Start of Japan's "lost decade"

**57. The 2001–2003 recession**
- Dot-com bubble burst: NASDAQ −78%
- 9/11 and corporate scandals (Enron)
- Mild in the US; stagnation in Germany and Japan
- Fed cut rates to 1% → housing bubble

**58. Unemployment and Okun's law. Rate and types of unemployment**
- Rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100%
- Frictional, structural, cyclical (+ seasonal, hidden)
- Natural rate = frictional + structural
- Okun's law: 1 p.p. cyclical unemployment ≈ 2–2.5% of lost output

**59. The nature and main types of inflation. Main price indices**
- Sustained rise in the general price level
- Creeping, galloping, hyperinflation (> 50% a month)
- Demand-pull, cost-push, structural, imported, wage–price spiral
- CPI (Laspeyres), PPI, GDP deflator (Paasche), core inflation; world inflation ~4.7% in 2026 (IMF)

**60. The concept and essence of global transformation of the world economy**
- Qualitative change toward one integrated world system
- Directions: digital economy, shift to Asia, value chains, multi-level regulation
- Drivers: ICT, liberalization, MNEs, cheap transport
- Now: fragmentation, tariffs, sanctions

**61. Interdependence is a major feature of the globalization process**
- Mutual dependence: events in one country affect others
- Channels: trade, finance, value chains, technology, migration
- Examples: 2008, chip shortage, 2026 oil shock (Hormuz)
- Asymmetry and weaponization of interdependence

**62. Globalization and regionalization**
- Globalization: single world economic space
- Regionalization: deeper integration of neighbours
- Balassa: FTA → customs union → common market → EMU → full integration
- Blocs: EU, USMCA, RCEP, CPTPP, Mercosur; trade creation vs diversion (Viner)

**63. Global challenges of the world economy**
- Between societies: war and peace, North–South gap
- Society and nature: climate, energy, food, water
- Human and society: demography, pandemics, poverty, migration
- New: digital divide, cyber, AI, debt; responses — SDGs, Paris Agreement

**64. STR (NTR) and global transformation of the world economy**
- Science becomes a direct productive force
- Stages: 1950s–70s automation; 1980s–2000s ICT; now AI and robotics
- Knowledge and data as key factors; value chains via ICT
- Automation weakens cheap-labour advantage → reshoring

**65. STRs and structural changes in the world economy**
- Agriculture → industry → services (about two-thirds of output)
- Knowledge-intensive industries; lower energy intensity
- Quaternary sector; manufacturing shift to Asia
- Energy transition; more trade in intermediate goods and services

**66. Ecology as a global problem. The main manifestations of exacerbation of the environmental situation**
- Climate change: 2024 first year above 1.5°C
- Pollution, deforestation, biodiversity loss, water scarcity, extreme weather
- Tools: carbon pricing, EU CBAM, green bonds
- UNFCCC, Kyoto, Paris Agreement; largest emitter China

**67. Competitiveness of countries. Criteria and assessments of competitiveness**
- Ability to raise productivity and living standards (Porter)
- Porter's diamond: factors, demand, related industries, strategy and rivalry
- Ratings: IMD (2026: Singapore, Hong Kong, Switzerland), WEF GCI, GII
- Institutions now matter most

**68. Factors of US economic development**
- Resources (shale oil and gas), territory
- Population and immigration
- Capital markets and the dollar; technology and universities
- Institutions, huge domestic market, secure geography

**69. Sectoral structure of the US economy**
- Services ~80%, industry ~18% (manufacturing ~10%), agriculture ~1%
- High-tech: chips, aerospace, pharma, cars, energy, defence
- Regions: Northeast, Rust Belt, Sun Belt, West
- AI boom: data centres, chips, power

**70. Characteristics of the activities of American TNCs**
- Largest source of FDI; $6.83 trn abroad (end-2024)
- Big Tech dominance; intangible assets
- Low-tax jurisdictions; global brands
- Trends: AI investment, supply-chain shifts, antitrust, tariffs

**71. The main problems of US economic development**
- FY2026 deficit $2.0 trn (6.2% of GDP); debt 100% of GDP
- Trade deficit $901.5 bn; record goods deficit $1.24 trn
- Policy uncertainty: tariffs, shutdown
- Inequality, health costs, dependence on Asian supply chains, AI bubble risk

**72. Structure of US exports and imports**
- 2025: exports $3.43 trn, imports $4.33 trn
- Exports: capital goods, energy, pharma, farm goods, services
- Imports: consumer goods, computers and AI servers, cars
- Partners: Mexico, Canada, China (deficit −32%), EU, Japan, Korea

**73. Economic development potential of Canada**
- 2nd-largest country by area; G7; GDP ~$2.2 trn
- Oil, gas, potash, uranium, metals, timber, water, hydropower
- Educated workforce; immigration
- AI research (Toronto, Montreal)

**74. Structure and dynamics of Canada's economic development**
- Services ~70%; mining and energy; manufacturing (cars, aerospace)
- 2025 GDP +1.7% — slowest since 2020
- Exports to the US fell under tariffs
- Problems: productivity, housing, household debt, US dependence

**75. Canada's foreign (economic) policy**
- US takes 71.6% of goods exports (2025)
- CUSMA, CETA, CPTPP; ~90% of exports to US stayed duty-free
- US declined to renew CUSMA on 1 July 2026 → annual reviews, expiry 2036
- Diversification to Europe and Asia (PM Mark Carney)

**76. The place and role of Japan in the modern world economy**
- GDP ~$4.2 trn; 4th–5th (India passed it in 2025)
- Leader in cars, robotics, machine tools, chip equipment
- Major investor abroad; lost "largest net creditor" to Germany
- G7; yen in the SDR basket

**77. Natural resource, labour, production and scientific and technical potential of the Japanese economy**
- Almost no resources; imports energy and raw materials
- ~124 million people, ~30% aged 65+; labour shortages
- Manufacturing ~20% of GDP; lean production
- R&D above 3% of GDP; chips revival (TSMC Kumamoto, Rapidus)

**78. Specific features and stages of development of Japan after the Second World War**
- 1945–52 occupation and reforms
- 1955–73 miracle (~10% growth, MITI, keiretsu)
- Oil shocks, Plaza Accord and bubble, "lost decades"
- Abenomics; 2020s inflation and rate hikes

**79. Structure of the economy of modern Japan**
- Services ~70%, industry ~29%, agriculture ~1%
- Cars, electronics, machinery, chemicals, steel
- Keiretsu, sogo shosha, SMEs as suppliers
- Weak consumption; exports drive cycles

**80. Japan's foreign economic strategy and its main features**
- From exports to investment abroad (investment income > trade balance)
- CPTPP, RCEP, EPA with the EU and UK
- Economic security; chips subsidies; China + 1
- 2025 US deal: 15% tariff + investment pledges

**81. Problems of Japan's economic development at the present stage**
- Ageing and shrinking population
- Highest public debt among developed countries
- Low growth (+1.1% in 2025), inflation above target, weak yen
- Energy dependence, US tariffs; PM Takaichi's ¥21.3 trn stimulus

**82. Germany's place in the world and European economy**
- 3rd economy (~$5 trn); 1st in the EU
- 3rd goods exporter ($1.76 trn)
- Largest net creditor (end-2024 data)
- With France — the engine of the EU

**83. Factors of economic development of Germany**
- Industrial base and the Mittelstand
- Dual vocational training; R&D ~3% of GDP
- Location in the EU centre; social market economy (Erhard)
- Euro; formerly cheap Russian gas (lost after 2022)

**84. Sectoral structure of the German economy**
- Manufacturing ~18–20% of GDP
- Cars, machinery, electrical engineering, chemicals and pharma
- Services ~70%; agriculture below 1%
- Industrial south and west; east still lags

**85. The main problems of economic development (Germany)**
- GDP −0.9% (2023), −0.5% (2024), +0.2% (2025)
- Expensive energy, car industry transition, Chinese competition
- Ageing, skills shortage, underinvestment, bureaucracy
- 2025 debt brake reform + €500 bn fund (Chancellor Merz)

**86. Germany's foreign economic strategy**
- Export-led: exports ~40% of GDP
- Partners: US, China, France, Netherlands, Poland
- De-risking from China (2023 strategy); energy diversification
- Support for EU trade deals; new focus on domestic investment

**87. France's position in the world economy and in the European Union**
- About 7th in the world, 2nd in the euro area
- EU founder; Franco-German engine
- Leader in aerospace, luxury, nuclear, tourism, agriculture
- Top European destination for FDI projects

**88. Industrial, labour, scientific and technical potential of France**
- Airbus, Safran, Renault, Sanofi, LVMH, EDF, TotalEnergies
- Nuclear ~two-thirds of electricity
- Educated workforce; unemployment ~8%
- R&D ~2.2% of GDP; France 2030; AI start-ups

**89. The current model of economic development in France**
- Dirigisme: strong state, state companies (EDF)
- Very high public spending and taxes
- Generous social model, 35-hour week
- Weakness: deficits, debt, slow reforms; growth ~0.8–0.9% (2025)

**90. Structure of the French economy**
- Services ~79%, industry ~17%, agriculture ~1.5–2%
- Aerospace, cars, pharma, chemicals, food, luxury
- Largest agriculture in the EU
- Île-de-France ~30% of GDP; Toulouse, Lyon

**91. Domestic economic policy and foreign economic strategy of France**
- Deficit 5.1%, debt 115.6% of GDP (2025); target 3% by 2029
- Political instability; pension reform conflict
- Strategic autonomy, Choose France, arms and luxury exports
- Protects farmers (against EU–Mercosur); Francophone Africa

**92. The UK's place in the world economy and its relations with the EU**
- About 6th economy; London — top financial centre (38% of FX)
- 2nd services exporter
- Left the EU 31 Jan 2020; Trade and Cooperation Agreement
- CPTPP (2024), India FTA (2025), US deal; EU "reset"

**93. Industrial, labour, scientific and technical potential of the UK economy**
- Pharma, aerospace and defence, cars, finance, creative industries
- Flexible labour market; weak productivity since 2008
- World-class universities; AI and life sciences
- North Sea oil declining

**94. Structure and dynamics of economic development (UK)**
- Services ~80%, production ~14%, construction ~6%
- 2008 crisis, austerity, Brexit, COVID, inflation >10% (2022)
- Growth 1.1% (2024) → 1.3–1.4% (2025)
- Problems: productivity, investment, debt, regional gaps

**95. Export–import structure of the United Kingdom**
- Services dominate exports; surplus in services
- Goods: machinery, cars, pharma, gold, fuels
- Large goods deficit; current-account deficit
- Partners: EU bloc ~40%, US largest single country

**96. General characteristics of the Italian economy**
- GDP ~$2.4 trn; euro area's 3rd economy
- EU's 2nd manufacturer; "Made in Italy"
- SME industrial districts
- North–South divide

**97. Factors of economic development of the Italian economy**
- Strengths: entrepreneurship, design, brands, tourism, Mediterranean location
- EU funds: NextGenerationEU ~€194 bn
- Weaknesses: few resources, ageing, bureaucracy, slow justice
- Very high public debt

**98. Structure and dynamics of economic development of Italy**
- Services ~74%, industry ~23%, agriculture ~2%
- Machinery, cars (Ferrari, Stellantis), pharma, fashion, food, ships, energy
- Miracle (1950s–60s) → stagnation since late 1990s → 2011–12 debt crisis
- 2025: GDP +0.5%, deficit 3.1%, debt ~137%

**99. The state's place in regulating the economy in Italy**
- History: IRI (1933–2002), ENI, Cassa per il Mezzogiorno
- 1990s privatizations
- Today: state stakes (ENI, Enel, Leonardo), golden power, PNRR
- Limits: EU fiscal rules and debt; Superbonus cost

**100. Italy's domestic and foreign economic policies**
- Meloni government: fiscal consolidation, tax cuts, PNRR reforms
- Support for the South (single special economic zone)
- Export surplus; partners Germany, France, US
- Energy hub (Mattei Plan); left the Belt and Road (2023)

**101. China's place and role in the world economy**
- 2nd economy (~$20 trn), largest at PPP
- Largest manufacturer and exporter ($3.77 trn); surplus $1.19 trn
- Largest trading partner for most of the Global South
- R&D 2.80% of GDP; reserves ~$3.4 trn

**102. China's economic development factors**
- Labour force and urbanization
- High savings and investment (~40% of GDP)
- State industrial policy; opening to FDI, WTO (2001)
- Export orientation, infrastructure, education, huge market

**103. Features of economic reforms in the PRC**
- 1978 Deng Xiaoping; gradualism
- Household responsibility; SEZs (Shenzhen 1980)
- 1992 socialist market economy; SOE reform; WTO 2001
- Since 2013: Belt and Road, Made in China 2025, stronger state control

**104. Structure of the economy of modern China. Regional policy**
- Services ~57%, industry ~36%, agriculture ~7%
- SOEs in strategic sectors; private firms create most jobs
- Weak consumption; investment fell 3.8% in 2025
- Coastal East first, then Go West, Northeast, Central; city clusters; hukou reform

**105. China's foreign economic relations**
- Trade 45.5 trn yuan (2025); surplus $1.19 trn
- Exports: electronics, machinery, EVs, batteries, solar
- Partners: ASEAN, EU, US (exports −20%), Japan, Korea
- Trade war with the US; Belt and Road; RCEP; yuan internationalization

**106. Peculiarities of China's economic development in the 21st century**
- 2001–2010 boom above 10%; 2010 No. 2 economy
- "New normal" 6–7% growth
- 2020s: property crisis, deflation, demographic decline, tech breakthroughs
- 15th Five-Year Plan (2026–30): self-reliance; 2026 target 4.5–5%

**107. General characteristics of NICs, common and distinguishing features**
- Asian tigers: South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore
- Later waves: Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia; China, India, Vietnam
- Common: fast export-led growth, strong state, education
- Different: Korea chaebol, Taiwan SMEs + TSMC, Singapore hub, Latin American import substitution

**108. NICs economic growth factors**
- Export-oriented industrialization
- Developmental state, five-year plans
- High savings, education
- FDI, export processing zones, US market, Japanese investment

**109. Dynamics of economic development and structural changes in NICs**
- Light industry → heavy industry → electronics → knowledge economy
- Asian crisis 1997–98; recovery
- Now high-income; Taiwan GDP per person passed Japan and Korea
- 2025: Taiwan +8.63%, Korea +1.0%; problems — ageing, one-sector dependence

**110. Foreign economic policy of the newly industrialized countries**
- Early: protect home market + promote exports
- Later liberalization: WTO, Korea in OECD (1996)
- FTAs: KORUS, Korea–EU, Singapore's network, RCEP, CPTPP
- Outward FDI; Singapore and Hong Kong top FDI hubs (2025)
